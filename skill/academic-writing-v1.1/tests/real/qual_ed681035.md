Abstract 
Non-classroom-based charter (NCBC) schools expanded rapidly in response to demand for 
flexible instructional models; however, literacy outcomes in these settings remained persistently 
low. Although literacy coaching is well established as an effective learning strategy in traditional 
school contexts, limited research had examined its application within NCBC environments. The 
purpose of this qualitative descriptive case study was to explore TK-12 independent study 
teachers’ and school leaders’ perceptions of an applied literacy coaching program within a 
Southern California NCBC school. Grounded in social constructivist theory, the study examined 
how literacy coaching was perceived in relation to instructional practice, collaboration, and 
organizational coherence across instructional modalities. Three research questions guided the 
study, addressing perceptions of (a) literacy coaching implementation, (b) its impact on 
individual instructional practice, and (c) its influence on collective instructional practice. 
Purposive sampling was used to select 15 teachers and school leaders engaged in a school-wide 
literacy initiative. Data were collected through open-ended questionnaires, field-based 
observations using reflexive notes, and document analysis. Data analysis followed Braun and 
Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis process, supported by MAXQDA software. Findings revealed 
convergent themes related to instructional coherence, professional collaboration, equity-oriented 
supports, and the practical constraints of implementation. Results indicated that literacy coaching 
was perceived as central to strengthening instructional coherence and professional collaboration 
in the NCBC context, with implications for leadership, instructional practice, and future research.  
Keywords: literacy coaching, literacy coach, literacy initiative(s), non-classroom-based 
charter school, pedagogy, scaffolding, social constructivist theory, zone of proximal 
development, independent study

 Chapter 1: Introduction 
Literacy coaching has a longstanding reputation as being an impactful strategy for 
improving instructional practice and student literacy outcomes (Reddy et al., 2025). This is 
especially salient in a moment when schools are continually faced with low literacy rates and an 
evolution of instructional modalities from completely in-person to varied hybrid and remote 
options (Anderson & Amesse, 2022). Across the U.S., schools have increasingly adopted literacy 
coaching to support teacher growth and provide students with enhanced literacy instruction 
(Green, 2024; Rowland et al., 2023). Concurrent to the adoption of literacy coaching, enrollment 
in non-classroom-based charter (NCBC) schools has steadily risen, driven by various reasons, 
though largely precipitated by parental demand for flexible, personalized learning models 
(Anderson & Amesse, 2022). Thus, the study of literacy coaching within the NCBC setting is 
salient in the current educational moment. Despite the expansion of NCBC enrollment many 
school systems lack defined frameworks for applying and evaluating coaching practices within 
these contexts. Given the relative dearth of existing literature specific to NCBC settings 
(Anderson & Amesse, 2022), a significant need exists, for further exploration to ensure literacy 
coaching practices are adaptable and effective in non-traditional, alternative learning settings.  
Parallel shifts in enrollment patterns have transformed how and where literacy instruction 
occurs (Green, 2024), prompting a need to understand the implications for coaching practice. 
While literacy coaching is an established method for improving literacy in traditional educational 
settings (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023), its implementation within NCBC settings remains 
underexplored. The combination of growth within the NCBC settings and persistently low 
literacy outcomes for students in these settings creates a need for targeted research. Despite their 
growth, NCBC schools have not received proportionate attention in terms of literacy coaching

(Anderson & Amesse, 2022), leaving a gap in understanding how coaching practices function in 
these unique learning contexts. These patterns also suggest systemic issues related to the 
professional learning opportunities afforded to educators in these respective contexts (Gallagher 
et al., 2023), as they are often expected to facilitate rigorous instruction without supports 
comparable to those found within traditional education settings.  
NCBC schooling contexts differ pedagogically from that which is found in traditional 
settings (Gallagher et al., 2023). Instruction within the NCBC setting tends to be highly 
individualized and supported by both synchronous and asynchronous instruction, which may 
occur in-person or virtually (Robertson et al., 2023; Shah, 2023). Teachers, students, and parents 
collaborate to facilitate educational planning, with the teacher playing the role of the credentialed 
expert (Gallagher et al., 2023). Given these distinct differences (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; 
Gallagher et al., 2023), the application of literacy coaching within the NCBC setting may not 
adequately address the needs of educators within these environments. Consequently, this 
exploration of how literacy coaching is implemented and perceived within the NCBC context is 
crucial to supporting improvements in both teaching and learning. 
This study explores teacher and school leader perceptions of an applied literacy coaching 
program in an NCBC TK-12 at a charter school in Southern California. The following narrative 
provides insight into the background and statement of the problem, purpose of the study, and 
significance of the study. Research questions are presented along with the theoretical framework 
underlying the study and a definition of terms used within the study. Assumptions, scope and 
delimitations, and limitations are also addressed prior to the chapter summary. Both, the 
established gap in the research, as well as broader literacy improvement agendas, which 
prioritizes equity for all learners regardless of their respective educational settings are aligned

with the aims of this study.  
Background of the Problem 
Many studies have demonstrated the positive impact of literacy coaching in relation to 
teacher practice and student literacy achievement (Abbot et al., 2024; Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 
2023). In a study conducted by Reddy et al. (2025), over 90% of literacy coaching programs 
examined studies showed improved outcomes. In traditional school contexts, literacy coaches 
engage in modeling, co-teaching, and the provision of timely feedback to facilitate dialogic 
support (Ciampa et al., 2023). The decentralized and asynchronous nature of NCBC models may 
limit the feasibility of such in-person, synchronous strategies (Gallagher et al., 2023). Coaches in 
these settings must rely on creative adaptations, such as virtual observations, asynchronous 
feedback loops, or remote modeling, the effectiveness of which remains largely undocumented 
(Green, 2024). Activities related to literacy coaching align with tenets of social constructivism 
and emphasize the construction of knowledge through social interaction (Vygotsky, 1978); 
however, the literature focused upon literacy coaching within the NCBC setting is significantly 
limited, in comparison. 
NCBC programs, including independent study and hybrid instruction, are characterized 
by their flexibility, personalization, and reduced in-person instructional time (California 
Department of Education [CDE], 2024d). These innovations, while promising, have not 
translated into measurable gains in literacy (CDE, 2024b), suggesting a disconnect between 
instructional design and student outcomes, which warrants greater exploration. While many 
NCBC schools have implemented literacy coaching programs as enrollment soared over 400% 
between 1999–2022 (Anderson & Amesse, 2022), the lack of consistency and structure in the 
application of coaching programs due to the highly flexible modalities of instructional offerings,

alongside the diverse methods for instructional delivery, have made it difficult to determine the 
effectiveness of literacy coaching within these environments (Dallavis & Berends, 2023). 
Statement of the Problem 
The problem is that TK-12 independent study students in a Non-Classroom Based 
Charter (NCBC) setting continue to demonstrate persistently low literacy outcomes, with recent 
data indicating that fewer than 25% of students in many California NCBC schools met grade-
level proficiency benchmarks (CDE, 2024b). National data also reflect this trend with only 31% 
of fourth grade students scoring proficient or above in reading on the 2022 NAEP assessment 
(National Center for Education Statistics [NCES], 2024). While literacy coaching is an 
established best practice within traditional learning contexts (Reddy et al., 2025), research about 
its application within the NCBC setting remains understudied and inconsistently implemented.  
Emerging research suggests that instructional coaching can significantly impact student 
literacy when sustained and embedded in instructional practice (Kochmanski & Cobb, 2023). 
Variable instructional modalities, lack of sustained coaching structures, and contextual 
differences unique to the NCBC setting pose challenges to effective coaching implementation 
(Gallagher et al., 2023). Without a greater understanding of how literacy coaching is perceived 
and applied in these settings, school leaders are likely to remain ill-equipped for efficaciously 
supporting teachers and improving student literacy outcomes. Understanding these perceptions is 
vital for designing coaching structures that are both sustainable and appropriately responsive to 
the needs of the NCBC context. The following section explores the purpose of the study in 
relation to this identified problem.  
Purpose of the Study 
The purpose of this qualitative, descriptive case study is to explore TK-12 independent

study teacher and school leader perception of an applied literacy coaching program within the 
Non Classroom Based Charter (NCBC) setting in Southern California. Additionally, the study 
seeks to explore teacher and school leader perceptions of the efficacy of this novel program 
across grade levels and learning modalities. This qualitative, descriptive case study seeks to 
identify both instructional and relational aspects of the applied coaching program, including 
interpersonal dynamics, trust-building, and communication strategies, which influence 
perceptions of literacy coaching efficacy. Data was gathered through instrumentation including a 
questionnaire, field-based observations, and document collection and analysis. A qualitative 
approach is appropriate because it enables the explorations of participant perceptions and 
experiences within the nuanced context of literacy coaching (Denzin et al., 2023; Ezer & Aksut, 
2021). Descriptive case study design is well-suited for examining bounded systems, such as a 
school site, in which the goal is to understand an event within a real-life context (Ezer & Aksut, 
2021; Yadav, 2022).  
Participants included 15 TK–12 teachers and school leaders currently engaged in a 
school-wide literacy initiative within the target NCBC school. Purposive sampling was used to 
select individuals who had firsthand knowledge of and experience with the literacy initiative and 
coaching program and could provide diverse perspectives based on grade level, role, and subject 
area. Participants were recruited through direct outreach in collaboration with site leadership, 
ensuring representation across grades and subject matter areas. Data was collected through three 
instruments: semi-structured questionnaires, reflexive notes from site-based interactions, and 
document analysis. Thematic analysis was used to identify recurrent themes and patterns across 
data sources. The study findings offer meaningful guidance for school leaders and policymakers 
seeking to implement and refine literacy coaching and initiatives in similar contexts, enhancing

both teacher practice and student outcomes. 
Significance of the Study 
Studying literacy coaching within the NCBC context holds potential significance for 
researchers, policymakers, and practitioners invested in literacy improvement and teacher 
development. By focusing upon the underexplored NCBC setting, this study seeks to provide a 
nuanced understanding of how literacy coaching operates within flexible, non-traditional 
instructional models. Findings may inform professional learning frameworks, support policy 
recommendations and positive social change such as state literacy improvement plans and 
improve coaching practices aligned with NCBC needs. Further, the study may influence 
educational equity by supporting improved literacy instruction in settings that cater to diverse 
and underserved populations, including English learners, students with disabilities, and students 
from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds (Pilotti et al., 2024). The study’s potential 
for positive social change is supportive of improving both student outcomes and overall quality 
of life for the long term (Shahidi Hamedani et al., 2024).

Theoretical Framework 
This descriptive case study is founded on the tenets of SCT, as established by Vygotsky 
(1978). In SCT, Vygotsky posits that knowledge and meaning are co-constructed through social 
interaction, which is mediated by language, context, and culture (Saleem et al., 2021). The zone 
of proximal development (ZPD) is a core principle of Vygotsky’s theory, referring to the gap 
between what a learner can accomplish independently, in contrast to what can be accomplished 
with support (Kolly-Shane, 2023). Literacy coaching is inherently relational and scaffolded, thus 
aligning well with SCT and the ZPD. 
In NCBC settings, instructional roles are often flexible and co-constructed, thus SCT 
provides an apt lens through which professional learning and support can be understood. It 
emphasizes the collaborative nature of coaching, recognizing both the coach and the teacher as 
contributors to the shared learning process (Taggart & Wheeler, 2025). SCT serves as an 
interpretive lens to understand how coaching is perceived and how it may be adapted to meet the 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  19 
unique dynamics within NCBC contexts. 
SCT, as a theoretical foundation, guides this qualitative, descriptive case study approach 
by informing both data collection and data interpretation processes. Understanding literacy 
coaching in terms of how knowledge is constructed through social interaction provides a 
framework for understanding teacher and school leader perceptions of literacy coaching. Further, 
exploring these perceptions through questionnaires, field-observation, and document analysis is 
supported in that it allows for gaining insights into participants’ language and personal 
reflections, central elements of the SCT. Guiding research questions focus upon how coaching is 
perceived and adapted in NCBC settings and the SCT framework supports the interpretation of 
how coaching interactions are indicative of relational professional learning. Likewise, thematic 
analysis is used to examine the co-construction of meaning across coaching experiences, 
emphasizing how context shapes implementation and outcomes.  
Definition of Terms 
This study employs various terms related to education and literacy coaching. The 
definitions for these terms may vary based on context and setting. The following definitions of 
key terms used within the study are provided to ensure clarity and consistency throughout the 
study’s narrative. Each definition for the following terms is predicated upon current research or 
legislative description. 
Literacy coaching is defined as a form of job-embedded professional development in 
which a peer coach supports teachers in improving literacy instruction through modeling, 
feedback, and collaboration (Green, 2024). 
Literacy coach is defined as a professional educator who enacts literacy coaching and 
supports instructional staff by providing ongoing, job-embedded professional development

focused on improving literacy instruction through strategies such as modeling, observation, 
feedback, and collaboration (International Literacy Association [ILA], 2017). 
Literacy initiative(s) are defined as the structured educational policies or programs, often 
implemented at the federal, state, or local level, which are designed to improve literacy rates by 
providing targeted support, funding, and instructional frameworks aimed at enhancing literacy 
achievement among students (Bogle, 2024). 
Non-classroom-based charter school (NCBC) is defined as a public charter school setting 
in California, in which less than 80% of the average daily attendance is generated through in-
person instruction (CDE, 2024a; 2024c). 
Pedagogy is defined as the action (the what) and the transmission (the how) of teaching 
within a specified context (Nicholson, 2022). 
Scaffolding is defined as the adapted support provided by the learning coach or teacher to 
help a learner progress toward independent competence (Johnsson et al., 2023). 
Social constructivist theory (SCT) is defined as a learning theory that emphasizes the co-
construction of knowledge through social interaction, language, and cultural context (Vygotsky, 
1978). 
Zone of proximal development (ZPD) is defined as the difference between what a learner 
can achieve independently versus what they can achieve with guided support from a more 
knowledgeable other (Kolly-Shane, 2023). 
Assumptions 
Assumptions are the respective beliefs an individual brings to their research based on 
existing experiences and perspectives (Creswell & Creswell, 2023). Qualitative research is 
likely to begin with assumptions resulting from existing, perspectival positionality (Lim, 2024). 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  21 
Within a study, assumptions must be addressed to alleviate researcher bias, and this can be 
accomplished through reflexive practice in which assumptions are bracketed, or set aside, to 
ensure increased objectivity (Ahmed, 2024).  
Bracketing, also known as epoche (Thomas et al., 2023), is employed to minimize bias 
stemming from existing assumptions. With bracketing, reflexive confirmability is increased, 
which further ensures reliability and objectivity of findings (Ahmed, 2024; Thomas et al., 2023). 
Through the temporary suspension of assumptions and presuppositions, research is conducted 
with an openness to the phenomenon under study (Thomas et al., 2023).  
The study assumed that participants would respond reflectively and truthfully about their 
experiences with the applied literacy coaching program. Likewise, it was assumed that 
researcher-participant relationships and power dynamics would be perceived as non-evaluative 
and peer-based. Further assumptions include that rapport would facilitate candor rather than 
socially desirable responses, participation would be experienced as voluntary and free of 
perceived pressure, and confidentiality protections and the separation of coaching and research 
roles would be understood. It was also assumed that the applied literacy coaching program had 
been applied with sustained consistency to the point of being able to provide meaningful data. 
Lastly, it was assumed that teacher and school leader perceptions can provide deep and valuable 
insights into the program’s effectiveness, which are necessary assumptions to support the 
descriptive case study design (Chowdhury & Shil, 2021). 
To bracket these assumptions, the study operationalized reflexivity through enumerating 
the separation of roles within informed consent, ongoing reflexive journaling tied to data-
collection and analytic decisions, deliberate attention to deviant cases within data-collection and 
constant comparison (Smith & Noble, 2025), member-checking on factual summaries, and 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  22 
maintenance of an audit trail (Russ et al., 2024; Thomas & Sohn, 2023). Confidentiality was 
ensured through pseudonymous identification codes with a separate linkage key. De-
identification encrypted and access-restricted storage additionally provided participant privacy. 
In alignment with this stance, non-leading observations were employed as were distinct 
communication for research interactions, along with the avoidance of coaching feedback during 
data collection (Thomas & Sohn, 2023). Additionally, participation for all individuals was 
entirely voluntary with no repercussion for non-participation, nor early withdrawal. 
Scope and Delimitations 
The scope of a study, or what is and is not addressed within a study, must be both clearly 
defined and manageable (Terrell, 2023), as it determines the breadth of topics accounted for 
within the study, established by the problem, purpose, and research questions (Yadav, 2022).  
In this study, the scope was limited to 15 TK-12 teachers and school leaders in southern 
California with experience in the research-site-based applied literacy coaching program between 
2023–2025. This site-based scope was selected to allow for in-depth and context-specific 
analysis and to ensure manageability of data collection and analysis. Focusing specifically on 
perceptions of the coaching model and respective literacy initiative, the scope of this study 
centered on relational and instructional perceptions, rather than student performance metrics. 
Research questions were specifically designed to align with this scope, ensuring data collection 
remains focused and relevant (Terrel, 2023; Yadav, 2022).  
Delimitations are the parameters established to narrow the study’s focus and define or 
describe the bounds of the study in reference to its scope (Terrell, 2023). In this study, several 
delimitations were established. First, only teachers and school leaders without experience with 
the applied literacy coaching program during these years were not included in the study’s scope. 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  23 
Individuals with experience outside of this timeframe were excluded to ensure contextual 
relevance and consistency of program implementation. Second, the study excluded perspectives 
from students, parents, or classified staff, as the focus remained on instructional and relational 
dynamics from the educator’s and school leader’s viewpoint. Third, only one NCBC site was 
selected, intentionally excluding multi-campus comparisons.  
These delimitations were necessary to support a focused, descriptive case study design 
and to allow deep exploration within a defined system (Terrel, 2023). However, such narrow 
focus may limit transferability of findings to other NCBC settings, especially those with differing 
student or teacher populations. Still, the study’s insights may be meaningful to leaders in similar 
charter environments, as it offers guidance for implementing relationally grounded, context-
driven coaching programs (Luft et al., 2022). 
Limitations 
Limitations include the constraints of a scientific study, which remain beyond the control 
of the individual(s) conducting the study and are, oftentimes, included as a component of the 
research methodology (Creswell & Creswell, 2023; Terrell, 2023). Because there are necessarily 
limitations of both qualitative and empirical research (Boswell & Babchuk, 2022), researchers 
are obligated to acknowledge these bounds to ensure future replication can also account for the 
same limitations (Creswell & Creswell, 2023; Montgomery, 2023). 
A focal limitation of the study was its focus on a single school site, which is restrictive in 
terms of the generalizability of findings (Zhou & Jiang, 2023). Additionally, reliance upon 
participant self-reporting may introduce unintentional bias or even limit insights into actual 
instructional change (Elston, 2021). The study also does not include a measure of student 
achievement data, which may constrain conclusions about the impact of coaching on literacy 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  24 
outcomes (Ciampa et al., 2023).  
Because qualitative research is understood as having implicit limitations (Ahmed, 2024), 
methodically applying measures to provide for trustworthiness within the study is critical to 
ensure analytically generalizable findings. The study included such measures for the assurance of 
trustworthiness as transparent data collection procedures, member-checking, and triangulation 
(Yadav, 2022). This study also systematically identified and interpreted thematic 
interrelationships across the collected data through analysis to further establish trustworthiness 
(Yadav, 2022). 
Chapter Summary 
Research into the nature of an applied literacy coaching program in the NCBC setting in 
Southern California is merited due to the continually lacking student literacy outcomes (CDE, 
2024b), despite various efforts aimed at improvement. As enrollment in the NCBC setting 
continues to expand, largely driven by family preference for individualized, home-based, and 
hybrid learning (Gallagher et al., 2023), educators and policymakers are continually confronted 
with students who continue to experience limited literacy success. Greater knowledge about 
literacy coaching in these settings provides insights into existing challenges, which currently 
result in less than favorable student outcomes.  
Guided by SCT, the study seeks to understand teacher and school leader perceptions of 
an applied literacy coaching program and the respective literacy initiative within a California 
NCBC school. The following review of literature provides insight into the current body of 
research concerning literacy coaching and related initiatives. Coaching models, theoretical 
perspectives, and the unique attributes of the NCBC environment are discussed through the lens 
of SCT, as is the impact literacy coaching has on instructional practice. This foundation provides

Chapter 2: Literature Review 
 Professional development offered through literacy coaching programs has been shown to 
be positively correlated with improved student literacy outcomes (Green, 2024). Because of this, 
literacy coaching has increasingly been adopted in professional development initiatives due to its 
documented impact on instructional outcomes (Rowland et al., 2023; Woodward & Thoma, 
2020). Enhancements in teacher competency resulting from literacy coaching are especially 
significant in settings with historically low literacy performance (Abbot et al., 2024). Teachers 
and school leaders also report positive professional and relational impacts that come from 
literacy coaching, including collaboration, trust-building, and shared instructional vision (Abbot 
et al., 2024; Wetzel et al., 2020). 
For decades, literacy coaching has been identified as an effective instructional support 
across both traditional and non-traditional contexts (Abbot et al., 2024). These educational 
settings include the non-classroom-based charter school (NCBC) settings which have continually 
experienced increased enrollment in recent years, driven by parental preference for personalized 
learning and instructional flexibility (Anderson & Amesse, 2022). While the value of literacy 
coaching programs has been well-documented in traditional educational settings, there is a gap in 
the literature concerning research about literacy coaching in the NCBC setting.  
The problem is that there is little research concerning the best practices for implementing 
an applied literacy program among teachers of grades TK-12, specific to the unique TK-12 
NCBC school setting in Southern California. Despite federal- and state-level literacy initiatives, 
the implementation of applied literacy coaching programs in these settings remains inconsistent 
and underexplored (CDE, 2024b). Challenges typical of NCBC settings, such as contextual 
differences in instructional delivery and a lack of sustained coaching structures (Green, 2024),

hinder the effective application of literacy coaching across grades TK-12.  
This qualitative, descriptive case study aims to explore TK-12 teachers' and school 
leaders' perceptions of an applied literacy coaching program within the NCBC setting in 
Southern California. The study also seeks to identify specific clinical and relational factors in 
support of applied coaching in the NCBC setting. Understanding these specific factors aims to 
create a rich understanding of the program’s perceived influence on teacher practice along with 
its perceived role in improving student literacy outcomes within the NCBC context.  
Knowledge concerning effective literacy coaching practices in NCBC settings is essential 
to support teacher learning and shared instructional improvement (Pilotti et al., 2024). Given 
enrollment projections, the lack of clarity regarding the best practices for the application of 
literacy coaching in NCBC settings presents a growing concern (Anderson & Amesse, 2022). 
Likewise, students within these NCBC settings, such as the target research site, have shown 
historical learning insufficiency (Dallavis & Berends, 2023), indicating further need for clarity in 
how best to support teachers.  
As a contribution to the knowledge base, the study provides a description of teacher and

the hope is that the results will lead to recommendations that can be shared with teachers and 
school leaders to improve practice and, if appropriate, also shared in professional settings at the

Theoretical Framework 
Theoretical frameworks extend the ontological and epistemological underpinnings of 
scientific research by offering a guiding paradigm for inquiry relevant for qualitative research 
(Boswell & Babchuk, 2022; Chafe, 2024). In this study, social constructivist theory (SCT) 
(Vygotsky, 1978) is the paradigm to guide the systematic exploration and interpretation of 
teacher and school leader perceptions of literacy coaching in non-classroom-based charter 
(NCBC) settings (Chafe, 2024; Luft et al., 2022). Attributed to Russian psychologist Lev 
Vygotsky (1978), SCT distinguishes itself from general constructivist learning theory by 
emphasizing the role of social interaction, culture, and historical context in human development 
(Haq & Sukinawan, 2024; Perkowska-Klejman & Górka-Strzałkowska, 2023).  
Social Constructivist Theory 
 The major theoretical propositions of SCT emphasize the active construction of 
knowledge and meaning making based on social experiences (Lare & Silvestri, 2023; Taggart & 
Wheeler, 2025). Within this theory, the assumption is made that knowledge does not exist 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  30 
independent of the learner (Haq & Sukinawan, 2024). Rather, knowledge is a social production, 
co-constructed through interaction and practice, such as in the case of the literacy coach-teacher 
relationship (Perkowska-Klejman & Górka-Strzałkowska, 2023). Within this social production, 
language and culture are the frameworks through which humans experience, communicate, and 
understand reality (Saleem et al., 2021).  
 Developed through intense education-based debates in the early twentieth century 
(Vygotsky, 1978), the zone of proximal development (ZPD) is a focal concept of SCT which 
holds to the tenet that a learner can gain new knowledge when linked to existing knowledge 
(Barrs & Richmond, 2024; Samanta & Mudi, 2024). When in the ZPD, the learner experiences 
stages of cognitive challenge in which there is disparity between independent ability and that 
which can be done with assistance (Kolly-Shane, 2023). As a result of this momentary 
dissonance, the learner finds the opportunity for the co-construction of knowledge in cooperation 
with more experienced learners (Wibowo et al., 2023), as is the case within the literacy coaching 
relationship. The ZPD has a scope, the lower end of which is determined by what the learner can 
do independently, while the higher end is predicated by what the learner can accomplish with 
assistance, encapsulated by the Russian term obucheniye (a combination of teaching and 
learning) (Barrs & Richmond, 2024; Kolly-Shane, 2023).  
Within the ZPD, the scaffolding process, borrowed from Wood et al.’s (1976) 
constructivism theory, is the assistance provided to learners as they work toward independence 
(Johnsson et al., 2023; Wibowo et al., 2025). Scaffolding refers to the strategic adjustment of 
support based on learner need (Kolly-Shane, 2023). Applied in various educational contexts, 
including literacy coaching, scaffolds provide guidance and support in improving practice 
(Gudmundsdatter Magnusson et al., 2023). Scaffolding is dynamic and adjustable support 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  31 
enabling learners to move into eventual autonomy, which is particularly relevant in professional 
learning contexts such as literacy coaching (Kolly-Shane, 2023).   
Vygotsky’s (1978) SCT emphasizes the foundational role of language and culture in 
shaping knowledge through social interaction (Saleem et al., 2021) in alignment with the 
problem and purpose of the study. From this perspective, professional learning emerges through 
social interactions situated in sociocultural contexts, which include perspectival activities, 
identities, and power dynamics as a theoretical lens for magnifying sociocultural phenomena 
(Lare & Silvestri, 2023), SCT is an interpretive framework for examining how the NCBC 
context shapes the application of literacy coaching. ZPD, central to SCT (Kolly-Shane, 2023), is 
especially salient, as it aligns with the function of literacy coaching as a scaffolded, job-
embedded professional support designed to improve instructional quality (Gallagher et al., 2023).  
This SCT framework provides a critical lens through which research relating to the 
application of literacy coaching within the NCBC context may be explored. Within this 
framework, teacher perceptions of how literacy coaching is enacted across varying instructional 
modalities, such as virtual, in-person, synchronous, and asynchronous (Anderson & Amesse, 
2022), may be understood as situated within a broader sociocultural learning environment. SCT’s 
emphasis on collaborative meaning-making also aims to inform the interpretation of school 
leaders’ perspectives on the systemic instructional influence of coaching practices. Anchored by 
Vygotsky’s (1978) concept of the ZPD, this framework positions literacy coaching as an 
interaction-driven and relational process that unfolds through social mediation (Saleem et al., 
2021). The interpretive stance is particularly relevant to NCBC settings, where instructional roles 
are fluid and co-constructed, and where coaching must adapt to a diverse array of learning 
configurations.

 Vygotskian SCT, including the concept of the ZPD and the derivative concept of 
scaffolding, is widely applied to guide pedagogies focused upon maximizing learner potential  
(Kolly-Shane, 2023; Wibowo et al., 2025). A significant area of SCT application within  
research includes teacher professional development in studies concerning professional social 
practice (Pilotti et al., 2024; Shah, 2022). SCT is also applied in pedagogical design for adults, as 
in literacy coaching feedback cycles (Taggart & Wheeler, 2025), and in learning scenarios which 
include the provision of feedback for learning (Lare & Silvestri, 2023). Notably, literacy 
coaching also draws upon these principles in terms of the relational aspect of the literacy

relevance in understanding how social interaction, collaboration, and mediated support facilitate 
learning and professional growth (Pilotti et al., 2024).  
Research Literature Review 
Enrollment in NCBC schools continues to rise in California and nationwide; however, 
student literacy achievement in these settings remains an area of concern (Anderson & Amesse, 
2022; Dallavis & Berends, 2023). To address persistent underperformance in literacy outcomes, 
educators across various learning environments have increasingly adopted literacy coaching as a 
targeted intervention (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023). A substantial body of research supports 
the efficacy of literacy coaching in traditional, in-person school contexts (Green, 2024). Yet, 
despite its growing use, the literature surrounding the application and perceived effectiveness of 
literacy coaching in NCBC schools remains limited in comparison.  
The Multifaceted and Evolving Role of the Literacy Coach 
Instructional coaching, including literacy coaching, has emerged as a widespread yet 
evolving form of professional development in U.S. schools (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023). 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  33 
Despite its increasing presence, literacy coaching remains relatively unregulated and subject to 
variation across educational contexts. Effective literacy coaches (LCs) must establish trust-based, 
collaborative relationships with teachers, which serve as the foundation for professional growth 
(Gudmundsdatter Magnussen et al., 2023; Weimann et al., 2022). Through sustained coaching 
cycles and embedded professional learning opportunities, literacy coaching is associated with 
improvements in teacher practice and instructional competence (Abbot et al., 2024). These 
outcomes align with social constructivist principles, which hold that professional knowledge is 
co-constructed in authentic, context sensitive interactions with more knowledgeable others 
(Vygotsky, 1978; Correnti et al., 2021; Gudmundsdatter Magnussen et al., 2023).  
The role of the LC is inherently multifaceted and extends across school, district, county, 
and even state levels. In a qualitative study of LCs, Woodward and Thoma (2020), suggested that 
school leaders and teachers found ambiguity surrounding the coach’s responsibilities that may 
hinder effective implementation. Although coaching is generally designed to support teaching 
and learning, LCs are sometimes redirected toward administrative or clerical tasks, which may 
diminish the ability to function as instructional partners. Ciampa et al. (2023) emphasized that 
LCs engage in a wide range of activities including modeling, co-teaching, conferencing, leading 
professional development, and sharing research. However, the fluidity of the role may lead to 
confusion and diminished relational efficacy if norms are not clearly established. SCT highlights 
the importance of role clarity and mutual respect in coaching interactions, as the co-construction 
of knowledge depends on defined but flexible social roles and shared goals (Pilotti et al., 2024).  
Coaches are most effective when schools establish professional norms that recognize the LC as a 
thought partner rather than an enforcer (Ruesink & Teichert, 2024). Administrators often view 
LCs as hope-builders, and coaches describe their work as dynamic and responsive (Gallagher et 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  34 
al., 2023). Ruesink and Teichert (2024) explored the experiences of first-year LCs and 
emphasized the centrality of relational work in coaching. Their findings suggest that new LCs 
should receive extensive preparation in adult learning theory, including strategies for positioning 
themselves as non-hierarchical partners when supporting adult learners. This aligns with SCT in 
terms of ensuring that coaching relationships are dialogic, reciprocal, and grounded in shared 
inquiry (Robertson et al., 2023).  
Troyer and Hemphill (2024) further documented the complexity of LC decision-making. 
Their study found that coaches’ enactment of their role varied widely, even among those trained 
similarly and working in comparable settings. Coaching strategies were influenced by individual 
belief systems, prior experiences, and contextual challenges. These findings reflect social 
constructivism’s assertion that learning (including teacher professional learning) is not universal 
or linear but, rather, is shaped by personal environmental factors (Taggart & Wheeler, 2025). In 
cases where teachers lacked prior literacy expertise, coaches often increased their level of 
support, which sometimes resulted in resistance. This dynamic again affirms the need for 
responsive scaffolding and co-constructed meaning-making in coaching relationships, rather than 
a rigid adherence to prescriptive models (Gallagher et al., 2023; Morgan et al., 2022).  
Legislative and Professional Underpinnings of the Literacy Coach’s Evolving Role  
Diversity in coaching responsibilities has been shaped, in part, by changes in federal 
education policy. Brieske-Ulenski and Kelley (2023) explain that while literacy coaching has 
existed for decades, its expansion was catalyzed by the Improving America’s Schools Act (1994) 
and the No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB) (2002). The Reading First initiative embedded within 
NCLB explicitly funded coaching positions in low-performing schools and mandated their 
presence in schools receiving federal literacy grants (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023). Over 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  35 
time, this legislation helped institutionalize literacy coaching as a formal role, albeit without a 
consistent national standard. Social constructivism helps explain how these legislative mandates 
translated into diverse, locally constructed interpretations of the LC role, shaped by institutional 
culture, community needs, and relational dynamics (Pilotti et al., 2024; Robertson et al., 2023; 
Shah, 2022).  
Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) (2015) reauthorized and expanded many of the 
professional development provisions of NCLB (2002), continuing the emphasis on literacy 
coaching as a strategy for school improvement. Bogle (2024) affirmed that high-quality 
professional development remains a key driver of reading achievement. Concurrently, 
organizations such as the International Literacy Association (ILA, 2017) refined their standards 
for literacy professional preparation, explicitly emphasizing disciplinary literacy, digital learning, 
and equity-informed instruction. These shifts broadened the LC role beyond compliance to 
include advocacy, collaboration, and the co-creation of socially just literacy environments. From 
a constructivist standpoint, this expansion reinforces the view of the LC as a guide who works 
within a socially negotiated professional learning space. As literacy coaching continues to 
evolve, the role of the coach must be understood not as static, but as one that is shaped through 
continual social interaction, adaptive expertise, and the mutual negation of meaning among 
educators (Robertson, 2023; Shah, 2022).  
Traditional Literacy Coaching 
Historically, literacy coaching has been implemented in a face-to-face modality in 
traditional, onsite settings (Wexler et al., 2025). Abbot et al. (2024) explored K-6 teacher 
perspectives on writing-focused literacy coaching in a traditional setting using a parallel 
convergent mixed-methods design, revealing that teachers who engaged in sustained coaching 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  36 
cycles characterized by collaboration, feedback, and access to new instructional strategies, 
perceived improvements in their teaching practice. Although the study was limited by sample 
size and partial survey completion, its findings suggest that traditional literacy coaching 
contributes to increased instructional competence and should receive greater attention from 
school leaders and stakeholders (Abbot et al., 2024). Within a social constructivism framework, 
these findings underscore the importance of dialogic, ongoing interaction between coach and 
teacher as a vehicle for the co-construction of pedagogical knowledge (Correnti et al., 2021; 
Shah, 2022).  
Mayuni et al. (2022) reinforced that traditional literacy coaching primarily focuses on 
supporting teachers in strengthening existing instructional skills, often through a structured 
coaching cycle involving pre-conferencing, observation, debriefing, and data reflection. These 
activities provide repeated, scaffolded opportunities for teachers to engage in situated learning, a 
hallmark of a constructivist model (Robertson et al., 2023). However, the authors also noted that 
the time- and location-bound nature of in-person coaching can limit its scalability and reach 
(Mayuni et al., 2022). While traditional coaching structures have long-standing efficacy 
(Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023), their impact depends on the coach’s ability to create shared 
meaning through personalized, context-responsive guidance.  
Troyer and Hemphill (2024) added that traditional coaching practices are often highly 
variable, even within the same district or training cohort, indicating that no universal model of 
traditional coaching exists. This variability reflects both a challenge and a strength when viewed 
through the lens of social constructivism (Pilotti et al., 2024). It suggests the situated nature of 
knowledge construction and the necessity of tailoring coaching interactions to the specific needs, 
experiences, and prior knowledge of individual teachers (Gudmundsdatter Magnusson, 2023).

Despite the widespread endorsement of traditional coaching, some researchers point to its 
potential limitations. Ruesink and Teichert (2024) cautioned that traditional coaching can 
become hierarchical, positioning the coach as the sole knowledge-holder and the teacher as the 
passive recipient. This dynamic contradicts the collaborative epistemology of implicit in social 
constructivism, that values the teacher’s knowledge, voice, and agency in the learning process 
(Saleem et al., 2021). When the coaching model lacks reciprocal engagement or co-creation, it 
may hinder authentic professional growth (Ruesink & Teichert, 2024). For this reason, 
constructivist-aligned coaching must emphasize not only literacy content, but also adult learning 
principles, cultural responsiveness, and relational trust (Morgan et al., 2022; Ruesink & Teichert, 
2024; Vygotsky, 1978).  
 Evidence continues to support the efficacy of traditional coaching for improving both 
teaching practice and student outcomes (Peters, 2022). Job-embedded, longitudinal coaching 
provides rich opportunities for iterative reflection and refinement (Ciampa et al., 2023). Brieske-
Ulenski and Kelley (2023) highlighted that in-person coaching contributes to deeper instructional 
improvement compared to coursework alone, supporting its continued use as a meaningful form 
of professional learning. As Woodward and Thoma (2020) note, literacy coaching offers 
practical, embedded support that enhances teacher practices and student literacy performance in 
observable ways.  
 Specific instructional practices are also enhanced when coaching includes modeling, co-
planning, and collaborative problem-solving (Gallagher et al., 2023). These coaching interactions 
mirror social constructivist tenets by embedding learning within authentic teaching contexts and 
providing structured opportunities for teachers to reflect, adapt, and engage in meaning-making 
through guided support (Shah et al., 2022). When framed as a co-learning process rather than a

top-down intervention, traditional literacy coaching becomes a powerful mechanism for 
collaborative professional growth and sustained instructional improvement (Wexler et al., 2025).  
Virtual Literacy Coaching 
 Traditional coaching is conducted in-person, whereas virtual coaching is mediated 
through digital technologies and online platforms (Rowland et al., 2023). Virtual coaching has 
evolved into a pragmatic instructional support model, complete with distinct methodologies for 
contextual understanding, instructional intervention, and relationship-building across distance 
(Rowland et al., 2023). Research confirms that teacher knowledge and instruction can be 
improved through both traditional and virtual coaching formats (Mayuni et al., 2022). 
Asynchronous and synchronous virtual coaching models are increasingly popular, offering cost-
effective and scalable professional development (Meyers & Washburn, 2022). As enrollment in 
non-traditional virtual and hybrid settings continues to rise, particularly in NCBC programs, 
virtual coaching has gained traction as a strategic support mechanism (Anderson & Amesse, 
2022).  
 While the goals of traditional and virtual literacy coaching are aligned in that they both 
seek to support teacher growth and improve student outcomes, the delivery mechanism differs 
significantly (Rowland et al., 2021). Virtual coaching now leverages technological tools such as 
video conferencing, shared digital documents, and recorded lessons to facilitate real-time and 
delayed instructional interactions (Peters, 2022). These tools allow additional methods of 
modeling, providing feedback, and participation in collaborative planning (Correnti et al., 2021). 
From a social constructivist point of view, these interactions continue to support co-construction 
of knowledge, particularly when the coaching relationship is dialogic and scaffolded to meet 
teachers in their ZPD (Vygotsky, 1978). Even asynchronous interactions, when intentionally 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  39 
designed, (Green, 2024) can serve as mediated learning experiences in which new instructional 
knowledge is built through structured feedback and shared reflection.  
Nonetheless, virtual coaching presents unique challenges (Peters, 2022). Coaches must be 
more deliberate in learning about classroom contexts, student needs, and environmental 
variables, all factors that are often more visible in face-to-face settings (Rowland et al., 2021). 
Trust-building in virtual coaching also demands greater intentionality, as relational connections 
must be nurtured through consistent and meaningful virtual engagement (Correnti et al., 2021). 
Modeling and observation may occur live or through pre-recorded video, enabling flexibility in 
scheduling but potentially diluting the immediacy of feedback (Peters, 2022). Despite these 
limitations, when carefully scaffolded, virtual coaching can still reflect core social constructivist 
values by fostering relationships and supporting individualized teacher learning within real-
world contexts (Gudmundsdatter Magnussen et al., 2023).  
One of the most frequently cited advantages of virtual coaching is its cost effectiveness. 
Virtual models are considered significantly less expensive than in-person coaching and can serve 
multiple teachers across different schools and districts (Peters, 2022). Full-time, resource-
intensive literacy coaching can cost between $3300 and $5200 per teacher while virtual coaching 
can increase the number of teachers assigned to one coach while also providing access to 
otherwise unavailable high-quality coaching in a cost effective manner (Kraft et al., 2012 as cited 
in Peters, 2022, p.23; Ciampa et al., 2023). This scalability increases access to specialized 
instructional expertise, particularly in under-resourced or rural settings (Ciampa et al., 2023). 
Furthermore, virtual coaching aligns well with NCBC and hybrid educational models that rely on 
distributed instructional networks (Kingsbery et al, 2025; Woodward & Thoma, 2020). When 
structured to preserve social engagement and feedback loops, virtual literacy coaching can 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  40 
provide equitable opportunities for professional learning that mirror the constructivist emphasis 
on interaction and situated knowledge building (Pilotti et al., 2024).  
The post-COVID educational landscape has shifted the nature of professional 
development (Imran et al., 2025) and only amplified interest in virtual coaching; however, 
research into its efficacy has yielded mixed findings. Cilliers et al. (2023) found that student 
literacy outcomes, particularly in oral language and reading, were less positively impacted by 
virtual coaching when compared to traditional methods, suggesting that accountability 
mechanisms may be more difficult to enforce in online environments. However, the authors also 
acknowledged that diminished outcomes may reflect low teacher motivation, rather than inherent 
limitations of the modality. In contrast, Myers and Washburn (2022) found that teachers engaged 
in virtual coaching gained proficiency with technology tools and became even more confident in 
navigating digital instructional strategies.  
Ciampa et al.’s (2023) qualitative study underscored the multifaceted nature of virtual 
coaching. Coaches reported adopting a variety of roles (technology mentor, academic guide, and 
emotional support provider), further illustrating how effective virtual coaching requires cognitive 
flexibility and deep content knowledge. From a constructivist perspective, this breadth of roles 
reflects the situated and adaptive nature of learning support (Gudmundsdatter Magnussen et al., 
2023). Coaches who can pivot responsively to meet teachers’ needs reinforce the social process 
by which professional knowledge is formed. Although limited research suggests that virtual 
coaching may be slightly less effective in certain contexts, the weight of existing evidence 
supports its capacity to foster teacher learning and, by extension, student success (Bennett et al., 
2023; Rowland et al., 2021; Weimann et al., 2022).  
Programs and Frameworks for use in Literacy Coaching 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  41 
 As a form of job embedded professional development, literacy coaching can be 
implemented through a variety of guiding frameworks, each shaped by the goals and context of 
the coaching interaction (Morgan et al., 2022). The development of a literacy coaching 
framework is an iterative process that must account for situational challenges, including 
institutional constraints, teacher readiness, and the evolving nature of instructional priorities 
(Gallagher, 2023; Wexler et al., 2025). These frameworks typically follow a continuum from 
clinical models focused on skill-building and data analysis, to relational models emphasizing 
dialogic interaction and trust building (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023; Wexler et al., 2025). 
Even non-literacy-specific models, such as the Targeted Feedback Model, may be adapted to 
supported literacy-focuses coaching by centering feedback, reflection, and continuous 
improvement (University of Washington Center for Educational Leadership, n.d.).  
Morgan et al. (2022) emphasized that the intent behind the coaching, whether to support a new 
curriculum implementation, refine instructional strategies, promote reflective practice, or 
catalyze transformational change, should determine the framework selected. From a social 
constructivist perspective, the choice of coaching framework influences not just instructional 
improvement but also the co-construction of professional knowledge (Pilotti et al., 2024; Shah et 
al, 2022). When coaches and teachers collaboratively reflect, problem-solve, and adapt practice 
based on real-world teaching experiences, they engage in the kind of socially mediated learning 
central to SCT and in this way, effective literacy coaching frameworks does more than to support 
technical skill acquisition; they foster situated learning through authentic, interactive processes 
that honor the social and relational nature of teacher development (Shah et al., 2022).  
Literacy-Specific Coaching Frameworks 
 Literacy specific frameworks, aimed at skills-based development, offer a structured lens 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  42 
through which literacy coaches can conceptualize and enact their roles. These clinical 
frameworks emphasize the improvement of teacher practice and data-informed decision-making, 
often relying on direct observation, modeling, and collaborative data analysis (Brieske-Ulenski 
& Kelley, 2023). Within these models, coaches work alongside teachers to apply literacy content 
knowledge in practical ways, co-analyzing student work and instructional strategies to refine 
practice (Green, 2024). From a social constructivist vantage point, this collaborative engagement 
represents a form of guided participation, where the coach, as the more knowledgeable other 
(Vygotsky, 1978), scaffolds teacher learning in the context of authentic instructional tasks. This 
type of coaching framework may involve the coach and teacher working alongside one another 
in the direct application of literacy knowledge and skills to provide feedback and co-create or 
analyze data. In any case, the emphasis is upon practical application (Ciampa et al., 2023).  
Wetzel et al. (2025) identified three overarching forms of coaching, each of which can 
serve as a foundation for a coaching framework. These coaching types include conformity, 
practice, and transformation. Coaching to conform centers on implementation fidelity (Robertson 
et al., 2023; Troyer et al., 2024), where the coach provides feedback to ensure adherence to 
specific standards or programs. Coaching into practice, however, encourages teacher growth 
through reflective dialogue, emphasizing self-assessment and metacognitive development. 
Coaching for transformation pushes further, inviting teachers to interrogate systemic structures, 
engage in critical pedagogy, and emerge as empowered change agents (Gallagher et al., 2023). 
The latter two coaching frameworks align with SCT by positioning the teacher as an active co-
constructor of knowledge and emphasizing the dialogic, social nature of professional learning.  
Non-Specific Instructional Coaching Frameworks 
In addition to literacy-specific frameworks, more general instructional coaching models

are often adapted to support literacy-focused professional learning. The includes constructs such 
as observation and targeted feedback cycles, which create structured opportunities for teachers to 
engage in self-reflection and dialogue with their coach (University of Washington Center for 
Educational Leadership, n.d.). The VECTOR framework, for example, employs a six-phase, job-
embedded professional development model facilitated through a virtual community of practice 
(Myers & Washburn, 2022). Teachers receive feedback on their writing instruction in a 
supportive, interactive environment. Within a social constructivist framework, the VECTOR 
model illustrates how mediated interactions in virtual spaces can still produce meaningful, co-
constructed professional learning experiences (Meyers & Washburn, 2022).  
  Ulenski and Van Allen (2019) highlighted the literacy collaborative as one of the earliest 
frameworks to systematically support literacy-focused coaching. Its foundational components, 
which include teacher inquiry, modeling, reflective practice, and collaboration, position both the 
coach and the teacher as learners within a shared professional meaning-making process. The later 
development of the Literacy Coaching Clearinghouse further institutionalized this collaborative 
model (Ulenski & Van Allen, 2019). These relationally grounded frameworks exemplify the 
social constructivist emphasis on co-learning and the importance of situated dialogue in the 
development of instructional expertise (Pilotti et al., 2024).  
Contemporary frameworks increasingly reflect a relational focus, recognizing that 
effective coaching is rooted not only in instructional knowledge, but also in the ability to 
cultivate trust, respect, and mutual engagement (Ruesink & Teichert, 2024). Collaboration

The Non-Classroom-Based-Charter Organizational Context 
The CDE (2024d) defines the NCBC schools in terms of instructional delivery, 
independent study compliance requirements, and methods of attendance accounting, including 
the calculation of average daily attendance (ADA) for funding allocation. At the state level, these 
school settings are understood as providing instruction outside of the traditional classroom 
setting through modalities such as home study, independent study, work study, and computer-
based education (CDE, 2024d). Specifically, a charter school in California is considered non-
classroom-based if less than 80% of its ADA is generated through traditional classroom-based 
instruction (CDE, 2024d). 
 California Education Code § 51745 (1989) outlines the core compliance requirements for 
NCBC programs. These include maintaining annual written agreements for each enrolled 
student, ensuring instruction is supervised by California certificated teachers, and aligning 
curriculum with grade-level standards. Following the pandemic, California further differentiated 
NCBC programs from traditional programs (CED, 2024c). While distance-based instruction was 
temporarily allowed in traditional public schools without adherence to typical compliance 
requirements, NCBC programs were still required to adhere to ESE (CDE, 2024c). These 
distinctions highlight the unique accountability frameworks governing NCBCs. .  
 Attendance in Californian NCBC settings is not based upon seat time, but on the teacher-
determined time value of student work products, which must be assessed by a certificated teacher 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  45 
(Cal. Educ. Code § 51745, 1989). This approach situates learning outcomes within the context of 
individualized academic engagement rather than classroom presence. From a social 
constructivist stance, this model reflects the theory’s emphasis on learning as a socially mediated 
process (Pilotti et al., 2024). Teachers, students, and families co-construct the learning 
experience by engaging collaboratively in the development, execution, and evaluation of student 
leaning, even in a flexible and asynchronous environment (Anderson & Amesse, 2021).  
The California State Board of Education (SBE) also requires NCBC schools to obtain a 
funding determination that verifies instructional practices, and operational structures align with 
legal mandates (CDE, 2024e). While the compliance measures serve to ensure educational 
legitimacy, they also shape the social and instructional roles of stakeholders within NCBC 
models (CDE, 2024d). In constructivist terms, the teachers function as a guide with greater 
experience responsible for the scaffolding of learning through the individualized guidance and 
evaluative feedback, while the parent acts a co-facilitator of the learning environment 
(Perkowska-Klejman & Górka-Strzałkowska, 2023).  
Despite the growing popularity of NCBC schools, a moratorium on the establishment of 
new NCBC schools has been in place since 2020 and is set to continue through 2026 (CDE, 
2024e). As the legal landscape continues to evolve, the social constructivist lens provides a 
critical perspective for understanding how instructional authority, learner agency, and 
collaborative meaning-making intersect within these on-traditional educational settings (Dallavis 
& Berends, 2023).  
Learning in the Non-Classroom-Based-Charter Context 
The NCBC environment offers a home study instructional model that mirrors some 
instructional elements of traditional homeschooling in California, though it remains distinct in 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  46 
both legal status and oversight (Dallavis & Berends, 2023). Unlike private homeschooling, 
which requires parents to file a private school affidavit, students enrolled in NCBC settings are 
legally classified as public school students (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; CDE 2024a). Most 
NCBC schools in California are not classified as online schools, and students are afforded 
significant flexibility through a personalized learning plan that is collaboratively developed by 
the credentialed teacher, the student, and the parent. This collaborative aspect illustrates the SCT 
principle of active knowledge building through shared experiences and dialogue within the 
learning community (Pilotti et al., 2024).  
Although parent involvement is essential (Pilotti et al., 2024), credentialed teachers serve 
as the instructional experts who determine the appropriate pedagogical approach (Robertson et 
al., 2023). As the credentialed experts, teachers typically identify the instructional pedagogy 
appropriate for each student (e.g. synchronous, asynchronous, virtual, in-person, etc.) (Dallavis& 
Berends, 2023). In terms of social constructivism, these multiple opportunities allow students to 
engage through socially situated experiences, where knowledge is co-constructed with others 
(peers, teachers, and parents), rather than passively received (Shah, 2022).  
Instructional practices in NCBC settings vary considerably and feature high levels of 
flexibility (Huang & White, 2023), though they are unified by the requirement that students 
many participate in synchronous, in-person instruction more than four days per week (CDE, 
2024d). As an alternative educational environment, also known as an independent study charter 
school or personalized learning environment (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; CDE, 2024d), NCBC 
settings afford students and families substantial flexibility with limited on-site attendance. 
Instructional approaches must be pedagogically adaptive and aligned with student-specific 
academic and developmental needs (Anderson & Amesse, 2022). These environments embody 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  47 
the core tenets of social constructivism by emphasizing learner agency, real-world relevance, and 
adaptive learner support (Shah, 2022).  
Anderson and Amesse (2022) specifically note that NCBC school models typically 
adhere to a home-based pedagogical approach which leverages school flexibility, student 
choice, and collaborative personalized learning plans to determine the best path of instruction for 
each individual student. Instruction is guided by teacher oversight but largely implemented in the 
home setting. Although parents play a significant role (Dallavis & Berends, 2023), many do not 
perceive themselves as instructional agents, leaving credentialed teachers responsible for 
aligning educational practices and student needs. As a result, credentialed teachers in NCBC 
settings often take the lead in aligning instructional philosophies with what will most benefit the 
individual student (Anderson & Amesse, 2022). This leads to a diverse range of instructional 
modalities, including small and large group instruction, virtual sessions, synchronous and 
asynchronous formats, and technology-supported platforms (Dallavis & Berends, 2023).  
Parent Preference for the Non-Classroom-Based-Charter Context 
 Awareness about the highly personalized nature of the NCBC model has increased over 
time, resulting in increased student enrollment. This trend accelerated in the aftermath of the 
COVID-19 pandemic with many families demanding personalized and flexible instruction 
(Anderson & Amesses, 2022). Families are drawn to NCBCs for a variety of reasons, most 
notably their combination of standards-based academic content and alignment with familial 
values and ideologies (Cheng & Hamlin, 2022). Many families select NCBCs to improve and 
reimagine their child’s education and in doing so the parent’s role within learning as a social 
mediated process is emphasized (Sssenkusu et al., 2022). Parents find educational agency in 
NCBCs where they can effectively serve as constructers of their child’s learning environment,

rather than passive bystanders (Shah, 2022; Mullen & Bartlett, 2024), .  
In their analysis of three national surveys, Cheng and Hamlin (2022) found that 73% of 
families cited dissatisfaction with some aspect of traditional schooling as a primary factor in their 
decision to pursue NCBC or home study alternatives, this indicates a sense of misalignment and 
disconnection between traditional schooling (Dallavis & Berends, 2023). This discontent spans a 
wide range of concerns, including instructional quality, school climate, and limited 
responsiveness to individual student needs (Cheng & Hamlin, 2022; Dallavis & Berends, 2023). 
Parents of children with special needs often seek the individualized supports and flexible 
structures available in NCBCs to create a sense of cultural and ideological congruence in their 
child’s learning environment enabling deeper student engagement and identity development 
(Edwards-Fapohunda & Adediji, 2024). Additionally, concerns about peer socialization and 
school safety, frequent topics among parents, have been cited as contributing factors in the move 
away from traditional public schools (Cheng & Hamlin, 2022).  
Some families prioritize curricular content, preferring learning approaches that reflect 
moral, religious, or philosophical values (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; Cheng & Hamlin, 2022). 
Other parents object to rote instructional practices and instead seek authentic, interest-driven 
learning experiences (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; Sssenkusu et al., 2022). A recurring theme 
across these motivations is the value placed on flexibility and parent-led decision-making, which 
NCBC models are uniquely positioned to support (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; Cheng & Hamlin, 
2022). Mullen and Bartlett (2024) highlighted the role of school philosophy and personal 
alignment in shaping school choice decision, emphasizing that families are increasingly selecting 
educational environments that reflect their beliefs and aspirations. Sssenkusu et al. (2022) also 
noted that the pandemic served as a catalyst for change; when parents became directly involved 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  49 
cofacilitators of learning, many sought redirect their child’s educational experience and offer 
their children a more holistic, personalized learning experience.  
The shift toward NCBC enrollment reflects a confluence of parental priorities, ranging 
from dissatisfaction with traditional schooling, the desire for educational personalization, and the 
appeal of flexible, values-aligned instruction (Huang & White, 2023). These motivations, 
intensified by learning disruption during the pandemic, reveal a broader societal trend toward 
customizable learning options (Dallavis & Berends, 2023). As NCBC models continue to grow, 
it becomes increasingly important to understand these parental motivations to inform the 
development of responsive and effective educational practices within the unique context 
(Anderson & Amesse, 2022).  
Research Topic Counterargument 
 While literacy coaching is widely recognized for its ability to enhance instructional 
practice and student outcomes, literacy coaching has been, in some circumstances, found to be a 
detriment to teachers and students (Robertson et al., 2023). For example, in a three year 
examination of the long-term impacts of virtual coaching programs in South Africa, there was no 
measurable improvement in students’ reading skills when compared to onsite coaching. In 
contrast, in-person coaching led to notable gains (Cilliers et al., 2022). Researchers attributed the 
disparity in outcomes to the greater efficacy of in-person interactions in developing complex 
pedagogical skills through in-person interactions, while virtual interactions were limited in 
cultivating the same skills (Cilliers et al., 2022).  
Additionally, the perceived efficacy of literacy coaching is not static and may shift over 
time, dependent upon several factors, including the intensity of the coaching support, clarity of 
roles and expectations, and the extent to which coaching is explicitly linked to student 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  50 
achievement (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023). In their exploration of educator perspectives 
about literacy coaching within a state-funded initiative, Woodward and Thoma (2020), in their 
study of educator perspectives within a state funded coaching initiative, noted that teachers were 
more likely to describe negative coaching experiences when literacy coaching involved low 
levels of support or when coaching lacked clear alignment with classroom goals. Teacher 
perceptions were also negatively influenced when the role of the coach was unclear (Brieske-
Ulenski & Kelley, 2023; Woodward & Thoma, 2020). Additionally, when there is a lack of 
clarity about the role of the literacy coach or disparity in the coach’s alignment with classroom 
goals, teacher perception of the literacy coaching relationship is negatively influenced, which is 
also the case when coaching activities are not explicitly connected to enhancing student 
outcomes (Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023).  
Though role ambiguity and alignment concerns are persistent negative influences over 
literacy coaching experiences, the use of technology in literacy coaching contexts also introduces 
new challenges (Peters, 2022). Michalik and Schermuly (2023), in their mixed methods analysis 
of technology-induced stress during literacy coaching, found that technology use can 
significantly contribute to emotional exhaustion and overwhelm for teachers in a coaching cycle. 
Their findings emphasize the importance of stress management and suggest that without stress 
management support, technology may hinder rather than help the coaching process (Michalik & 
Schermuly, 2023). Teacher resistance can also undermine the effectiveness of literacy coaching. 
In their qualitative exploration of discourse between teachers, literacy coaches, and a school 
principal Robertson et al. (2023) found that teacher resistance to literacy coaching primarily 
originated from teachers’ perceived lack of agency, limited understanding of subject matter 
content, and misalignment between the coach’s role and the classroom context. This resistance 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  51 
intensified when coaches were unresponsive to teacher concerns. The authors encourage future 
research into coaching dialogue to encourage greater teacher autonomy during the coaching 
process (Robertson et al., 2023).  
These research findings complicate the widely accepted narrative about literacy coaching 
being universally beneficial. Suggesting instead, that the success of literacy coaching programs 
depends upon contextual factors, including delivery mode, sustained support, role clarity, 
relational alignment, and cognitive demands. When these needs are not addressed, coaching can 
result in teacher disengagement, increased stress, minimal instructional improvement (Ciampa et 
al., 2023). Nevertheless, this limited body of counter-research does not negate the demonstrated 
success of literacy coaching. Rather, the importance of contextual sensitivity, relational 
responsiveness, and fidelity in implementation (Kingsbery et al., 2025). Resultantly, more 
research into the nature of literacy coaching across various contexts is necessary to account for 
experiential variability, such as that within the NCBC school environment.  
Chapter Summary 
Existing literature emphasizes the evolving role of literacy coaching as well as the impact 
literacy coaching has made within traditional and virtual school settings. However, most current 
research to date primarily addresses in-person, traditional school environments. Applied literacy 
coaching programs, across their various forms and frameworks, are consistently linked to 
improved teacher performance, enhanced student reading outcomes, and overall stronger 
instructional practice.  
While the documented benefits of both traditional and virtual literacy coaching are 
evident (Cilliers et al., 2022), a notable gap is apparent in research concerning the application of 
literacy coaching within NCBC environments. As enrollment within NCBC school settings

Methodology 
Qualitative research is a method that allows for an in-depth engagement in understanding 
perceptions of phenomena, which makes it an appropriate methodology for the study (Flick,

interactive method of research and complex interpretive practice (Denzin et al., 2023). 
Qualitative research is advantageous in that it ensures deep and rich knowledge is discerned from 
the data collected (Ezer & Aksut, 2021).  
Design 
Case study design brings forth rich data concerning the facets of the topic under purview 
and exploration without clearly defined common protocols (Ezer & Aksut, 2021; Yadav, 2022). 
This type of case study design allows for the gathering of rich data concerning the perceptions of 
the study participants in relation to the applied literacy coaching program. The gathering of such

 Descriptive case study design is known as a purely qualitative design that provides depth

Data Analysis 
Qualitative data analysis (QDA) is typically described in terms of non-linearity and 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  65 
iteration (Lester et al., 2020). QDA requires the continual systematic analysis of aggregate 
textual data, such as collecting questionnaire responses and content analysis in this study (Naeem 
et al., 2023). Thematic analysis is a flexible method for QDA of data collection, providing for 
identifying and coding themes in alignment with the research questions (Xu & Zammit, 2020). 
This study employed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-step thematic analysis method.  
Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-step method began with data familiarization.In this study 
data familiarization occurred through aggregating written responses from open-ended 
questionnaires, written field observation notes, and collected documents and reading through 
each multiple times to discern central ideas (Xu & Zammit, 2020). This process ensured a high 
level of familiarity with the data prior to formal coding and establishing initial codes (Braun & 
Clarke, 2006).  
Secondarily, in thematic coding is the generation of initial codes to systematically 
establish meaningful units of data by coding interesting data features and collating data relevant 
to each code (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Axial coding was implemented prior to establishing initial 
themes and patterns (Naeem et al., 2023). MAXQDA software supported the organization of 
initial codes and provided a structured and iterative approach to data collation (Rädiker & Gizzi, 
2024).  
Thirdly, the search for themes occurred through collating initial codes into potential 
themes and gathering relevant to each theme (Braun & Clarke, 2006). In this step, axially coded 
data was reviewed to establish initial themes and patterns in a goal-free, inductive approach 
(Naeem et al., 2023, para. 2). Data relevant to each theme was also categorized using the 
MAXQDA software (Rädiker & Gizzi, 2024).  
The fourth step of thematic analysis involves reviewing themes, making iterative 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  66 
comparisons, and refining emergent themes that support the conceptualization of identified 
themes (Braun & Clarke, 2006). This step was accomplished by checking to determine alignment 
between themes, coded units of meaning, and the entirety of the dataset (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 
2021). MAXQDA software was employed to generate a thematic map (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 
Rädiker & Gizzi, 2024).  
After review, comparison, and refinement the fourth step of defining and naming themes, 
requires ongoing analysis to refine the specific components of each identified theme (Braun & 
Clarke, 2006). At this point, MAXQDA software was employed to generate clear names for each 
theme and clarify the specifics for each determined theme (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Rädiker & 
Gizzi, 2024). This step resulted in naming clear themes relevant to the dataset.  
 A report was produced in the sixth and final step of this thematic analysis procedure 
(Braun & Clarke, 2006). This step provided an opportunity for utilizing the MAXQDA software 
to further categorize data into themes and select appropriate extracts (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 
Rädiker & Gizzi, 2024). The final report presented themes in a cohesive narrative aligned with 
the research questions. Representative participant and document-based quotations were included 
to support interpretations and the credibility of each theme (Clarke, 2006; Naeem et al., 2023).

Trustworthiness and establishing reliability and validity through rigor increased

the measure of research soundness as related to the veracity of the encompassing process 
pertaining to the measurement of the research questions (Hayashi et al., 2021). In qualitative 
research, reliability refers to the consistency and replicability of the research process and results 
(Lim, 2024). Reliability and validity are foundations for credibility, dependability,

transferability, and confirmability (Yadav, 2022). Reliability and validity are compromised when

Transferability 
Transferability in qualitative research was the process used to apply findings from a study 
of a specific context to a different context, lending to generalizability (Drisko, 2024; Lim, 2024).

the NCBC research context and focal applied literacy program, along with a purposive sampling 
of participants with experience with the applied coaching program, served to establish 
transferability.

participants with a request to confirm the context of the observation supported credibility, as did

Chapter 4: Research Findings and Data Analysis Results 
Literacy coaching has a long-standing reputation for improving instructional practices 
and student literacy outcomes, particularly in traditional learning environments (Abbot et al., 
2024; Brieske-Ulenski & Kelley, 2023; Ciampa et al., 2023; Reddy et al., 2025). However, the 
educational landscape has evolved in non-classroom-based-charter schools, driven by a demand 
for flexible learning models (Anderson & Amesse, 2022; California Department of Education 
[CDE], 2024b). Despite the documented value of literacy coaching in traditional settings, there is 
a significant need for further exploration into how effective literacy coaching practices can be 
adapted and applied within these unique, non-traditional environments. Existing research shows 
positive impacts of literacy coaching programs in traditional schools, with over 90% 
demonstrating improved outcomes (Reddy et al., 2025), but the decentralized and asynchronous 
natures of NCBC models may limit the feasibility of traditional in-person strategies, making 
creative adaptations necessary, but largely undocumented (Dallavis & Berends, 2023).  
The problem is that TK-12 independent study students in a Non-Classroom Based 
Charter (NCBC) setting continue to demonstrate persistently low literacy outcomes, with recent 
data indicating that fewer than 25% of students in many California NCBC schools met grade-
level proficiency benchmarks (CDE, 2024a). The purpose of this qualitative, descriptive case 
study is to explore TK-12 independent study teacher and school leader perception of an applied 
literacy coaching program within the Non Classroom Based Charter (NCBC) setting in Southern 
California. There are three research questions guiding this study.  
Research Question 1: How do teachers, who teach TK-12 independent study, perceive the 
nature of the school-wide literacy coaching program application in the TK-12, NCBC school 
setting in California?

Research Question 2: How do teachers, who teach TK-12 independent study, perceive the 
impact of literacy coaching upon their individual instructional practice across various modalities 
within the TK-12, NCBC school setting in California?  
Research Question 3: How do school leaders, who lead teachers of TK-12 independent 
study, perceive the impact of literacy coaching upon collective instructional practice across 
various modalities within the TK-12, NCBC school setting in California? 
This qualitative descriptive case study explores TK-12 teacher and school leader

Building Teacher Capacity through Coaching 
 Study outcomes showed that literacy coaching was perceived as a primary method for 
building teachers’ instructional capacity in the NCBC setting by supporting reflective dialogue, 
instructional modeling, and targeted feedback across content areas and modalities. Abbot et al. 
(2024) found that literacy coaching strengthened teacher competency by supporting guided 
reflection, instructional modeling, and feedback cycles that increased instructional ability in 
traditional school settings. Similarly, Rowland et al. (2023) reported that sustained coaching 
contributed to improved teacher decision-making and intentional use of instructional strategies 
across content areas. Consistent with these findings, teachers perceived the applied literacy 
coaching program as a structured professional support that strengthened their instructional 
practice through reflective dialogue, modeling, and targeted feedback. Participants described 
coaching as clarifying instructional expectations and deepening their ability to integrate literacy 
strategies across subject areas, indicating that coaching functioned as a capacity-building 
structure rather than a compliance-driven initiative.  
As understood through SCT (Vygotsky, 1978), the coaching relationship in this study 
reflected a dialogic and scaffolded learning process, in which interaction with a more 
knowledgeable other supported teacher development within a zone of proximal development 
(ZPD). While prior research has examined these relationships in traditional, site-based 
environments, this study extends the literature by demonstrating that capacity-building through 
literacy coaching can also occur in hybrid and independent study contexts. Despite greater 
teacher autonomy and less direct peer interaction (Anderson & Amesse, 2022), participants 
perceived coaching as an effective method of socially mediated instructional growth.

Viewed through the lens of SCT, the emergence of a shared literacy framework reflects

explored coherence within traditional, site-based school models, this study extends the literature

Fostering a Culture of Reading and Engagement

being supportive in the development of a school-wide culture of reading and engagement,

traditional educational settings. Congruent with these findings, this study found that meaningful 
shifts in school-wide attitudes toward literacy were described by participants, who noted greater 
student engagement with reading and a stronger organizational emphasis on literacy as a shared 
cultural value. Teachers in this study observed students interacting with texts more consistently 
and approaching literacy tasks with increased confidence, suggesting that the initiative fostered 
not only instructional change but also a broader cultural transformation.  
 When considered within the context of SCT, these changes reflect socially mediated 
cultural development, in which repeated interactions between teachers, leaders, students, and 
literacy coaches, reinforce new organizational norms and expectations. Because past research has 
primarily centered on such cultural shifts within traditional school settings, this study extends the 
existing knowledge base by showing how a literacy-centric culture can emerge in NCBC 
settings, where staff and students are geographically dispersed and learning frequently occurs 
outside of traditional classroom structures. The development of a literacy culture in this 
environment underscores the role of consistent, school-wide messaging and relational alignment

Navigating Time and Implementation 
 This study also found that while teachers largely perceived literacy coaching as 
meaningful and professionally valuable, their ability to fully engage in coaching activities was

constraints significantly shaped the depth and sustainability of coaching implementation in the 
NCBC setting. Ciampa et al. (2023) indicated that time constraints and competing instructional

without protected time, coaching initiatives may struggle to achieve sustained instructional 
impact. Consistent with this research, time emerged as one of the most significant contextual

engagement with the coaching process.

dialogue, reflection, and shared problem-solving, and these processes are jeopardized when time 
is fragmented. In NCBC environments, where asynchronous instruction and flexible instruction 
is the standard, these challenges become magnified. This study extends the existing literature by 
highlighting the necessity of intentional organizational structures that preserve time for 
collaborative learning, to provide the infrastructure for coaching to reach its full potential in 
hybrid and independent study instructional models.

strategies, and UDL structures assists in meeting the needs of diverse learners throughout the 
NCBC setting. Pilotti et al. (2024) reported that coaching initiatives grounded in inclusive 
instructional frameworks enhanced teachers’ capacity to address learner variability. Similarly, 
Green (2024) found that literacy coaching supported teachers’ ability to implement differentiated 
instruction and scaffolding processes that improved access to literacy learning for varied student 
populations. Congruent with these findings, this study found that teachers and school leaders 
perceived equity as a central benefit of the literacy coaching initiative. Participants emphasized 
that coaching helped them use scaffolds, differentiated practices, and Universal Design for 
Learning structures to meet the needs of diverse learners. Teachers also described increased 
intentionality in designing accessible learning experiences, particularly for students requiring 
additional support. 
 In alignment with SCT, differentiated support reflects the core principle that learning 
occurs when intervention is tailored to the learner’s developmental needs and supported within 
the ZPD (Vygotsky, 1978). This study extends the literature by illustrating how equity-centered 
coaching approaches can be effectively translated into NCBC contexts, where instruction occurs 
across multiple modalities and individualized instructional pathways. Participant perceptions 
indicate that the coaching program helped teachers design accessible learning experiences across 
contexts, thus extending the literature by illustrating how coaching practices centered on student 
equity can be translated into flexible learning environments.  
 Across all themes relevant to the study’s findings, participants perceived the applied 
literacy coaching program as contributing to increased coherence, instructional capacity, cultural

alignment, and student access. Literacy coaching functioned as a socially mediated, dialogic 
learning structure consistent with the tenets of SCT, allowing teachers and leaders to 
collaboratively construct shared meaning for improved practice. While the program was 
perceived as highly valuable, time and workload emerged as consistent considerations shaping 
the depth and sustainability of implementation. Collectively, the findings confirm that literacy 
coaching shows promise in NCBC settings and offer insight into how coaching models may be 
adapted and supported in dynamic and non-traditional learning models.  
Limitations 
 Several limitations, or constraints beyond the control of the researcher influenced the 
trustworthiness of this study, necessitating acknowledgement for future replication (Creswell & 
Creswell, 2023; Montgomery, 2023; Terrell, 2023). The single-site design and small sample size 
of 15 teachers and school leaders may limit the ability to apply findings across a broader 
population. However, transferability was strengthened through thick description (Lim, 2024), 
purposive sampling, and iterative thematic data analysis to providing sufficient contextual detail 
to support application in comparable educational settings.  
 Credibility was established through member-checking, triangulation, and reflexive 
practice. Participants reviewed and confirmed the accuracy of their questionnaire responses and 
reflexive notes to ensure fidelity of alignment between researcher interpretation and participant 
expression (Lim, 2024). Triangulation across questionnaires, field-based observations, and 
organizational documents further strengthened credibility and supported transferability to 
comparable contexts (Yadav, 2022). Dependability was supported through consistent and aligned 
data collection and analysis procedures, reinforced by a transparent audit trail, analytic 
refinement, and reflexive journaling(Lim, 2024). While variability was introduced through 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  105 
scheduling and respective teachers’ workloads, systematic documentation and triangulation 
promoted consistency and stability, resulting in coherent findings supportive of transferability.  
 Confirmability was strengthened through bracketing, a transparent audit trail, and 
reflexive journaling to mitigate potential researcher bias related to prior experience with the 
research site (Ahmed, 2024). Member-checking further grounded interpretations in participant 
expression. The combination of bracketing, reflexivity, and documentation supported objective 
confirmability of the study’s findings, lending to future contextual application.  
Recommendations for Future Research 
 Research on literacy coaching in NCBC settings remains limited, despite continual 
growth and a documented need for instructional coherence in these flexible learning models 
(Anderson & Amesse, 2022; Gallagher, 2023). The findings of this descriptive case study present 
several targeted opportunities for future research related to literacy coaching in NCBC contexts. 
In alignment with the methodological framework, the following three recommendations have 
emerged directly from analysis of the study’s findings and focus on areas most crucial to 
advancing understanding of literacy coaching in NCBC contexts (Creswell & Creswell, 2023).  
Future research should explore literacy coaching across multiple NCBC sites to 
determine whether the instructional and organizational patterns identified in this study are 
consistent across diverse non-traditional settings. Participants in this study perceived coaching as 
strengthening instructional coherence, shared language, and collective responsibility for literacy; 
however, these findings were derived from a single-site case. Comparative multi-site qualitative 
or mixed-methods studies would allow research to explore how coaching structures operate 
across NCBC contexts with different program models, staffing patterns, or instructional 
expectations. Such research would extend current findings by identifying contextual conditions 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  106 
under which literacy coaching is most effective and sustainable in NCBC environments, while 
additionally lending to future policy-based decision-making at the local, state, and national 
levels.  
 Likewise, future research examining the relationships between literacy coaching and 
student literacy achievement is needed. Because the current study focused on perceptions, 
empirical investigation incorporative of student reading performance, growth data, or 
longitudinal literacy trends would help clarify which coaching components are most strongly 
associated with improved student outcomes in flexible learning environments. Such inquiry 
would build on current research suggesting that coaching contributes to improved literacy 
outcomes in traditional settings (Rowland et al., 2023; Woodward & Thoma, 2020) and address 
an existing gap in NCBC literature.  
 Longitudinal research is also merited to understand how coaching influences instructional 
culture and coherence over NCBC settings. Findings from this study revealed that time 
constraints, workload demands, and structural features of flexible instruction influenced 
participants’ ability to fully engage in coaching. Longitudinal studies could investigate whether 
coaching leads to sustained improvements in collaborative practice, literacy culture, and 
instructional coherence, or whether patterns of teacher turnover, hybrid scheduling, or 
instructional isolation interrupt long-term gains. Sustained research would provide valuable 
insight into how coaching models can be adapted to support continuity and instructional stability 
in non-traditional settings.  
Implications for Leadership 
 The findings of this study hold several implications for school and district leaders 
working in NCBC settings, particularly those implementing literacy and coaching initiatives

across distributed and flexible instructional environments. Analysis of teacher and school leader 
perceptions indicated that leadership decisions related to time allocation, instructional coherence, 
and equity directly shaped the perceived effectiveness and sustainability of the literacy coaching 
program. The following three implications are founded directly in the study’s findings and 
highlight leadership actions that can strengthen literacy coaching implementation in NCBC 
contexts. 
A key implication for leaders in NCBC settings relates to the importance of protecting 
time for literacy coaching. While participants perceived literacy coaching as a meaningful form 
of professional support, time constraints and workload demands frequently limited sustained 
engagement in coaching cycles. These perceptions reflect current research demonstrating that 
insufficient time allocation jeopardizes coaching effectiveness (Ciampa et al., 2023). Leaders 
play an instrumental role in designing organizational structures that safeguard time for coaching-
related collaboration, reflection, and instructional planning, signaling that literacy is central to 
the school’s mission and supporting coaching as a sustained form of professional learning. 
 Another implication for leadership concerns sustaining unified literacy frameworks. 
Participants noted that shared language, aligned tools, and coherent expectations greatly 
strengthened instructional consistency. These insights align with studies indicating that 
coherence is foundational to literacy improvement (Green, 2024; Woodward & Thoma, 2020). 
Leaders play a critical role in reinforcing these structures through consistent messaging, ongoing 
training, and integrated materials across grades and modalities, especially in NCBC 
environments, where autonomy and instructional variation are common.  
 An additional implication for school leadership is the promotion of equitable access to 
literacy-based instruction. Participants described how coaching supported differentiated

instruction and the integration of Universal Design for Learning. This aligns with research that 
literacy coaching contributes to equitable instructional practice (Pilotti et al., 2024). Leaders are 
positioned to ensure teacher access to resources, training, and assessment tools that support 
diverse learners, particularly in NCBC settings where student pathways may vary significantly, 
thereby strengthening inclusive literacy instruction and addressing persistent literacy gaps and 
contributing to positive organizational and educational social change rooted in equitable literacy 
practice.  
 Together, these implications illustrate how leadership practices can strengthen literacy 
coaching in NCBC settings. Such leadership practices, including time allocation, coherence-
building, and equity-centered planning, have the capacity to generate meaningful and positive 
change linked to sustainability in instructional improvement, enhanced professional capacity, and 
improved student access to literacy learning. With apt attention to these implications, leaders are 
likely to experience improvements in professional capacity and literacy success. Collectively, 
these implications reflect actionable leadership practices grounded in the study’s findings and 
aligned with the demands of flexible NCBC instructional environments.  
Conclusion 
 This chapter provided an integrated interpretation of the findings derived from the 
qualitative descriptive case study, which explored how teachers and school leaders in a TK–12 
NCBC school perceived an applied literacy coaching program and its related literacy initiative. 
The analysis of questionnaire responses, reflexive observation notes, and organizational 
documents revealed five interconnected themes illustrating how coaching influenced 
instructional practice, coherence, school-wide culture, implementation processes, and equitable 
access. These themes illuminated the nuanced ways coaching supported professional growth, 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  109 
strengthened alignment across instructional modalities, and contributed to a literacy-centered 
organizational identity within the NCBC context. 
 As understood through the social constructivist perspective, literacy coaching operated as 
a socially mediated, dialogic structure in which teachers engaged in sustained reflection and 
shared meaning-making. Such interactions supported the co-construction of instructional, 
literacy-based knowledge and reinforced the importance of relational and contextual factors on 
teacher development. The findings indicated that coaching created opportunities for teachers to 
refine practice, deepen their understanding of literacy, and access responsive and differentiated 
supports for diverse learners. These results align with research describing coaching as a catalyst 
for instructional improvement (Abbot et al., 2024; Rowland at al., 2023) and extend the literature 
by demonstrating how coaching can be meaningfully adapted to non-traditional NCBC settings 
(Anderson & Amesse, 2022; Green, 2024).  
 The study also confirmed that contextual obstacles to the literacy coaching and related 
initiative, including time constraints, workload demands, and varied instructional schedules, 
shaped the degree to which teachers could effectively engage in coaching. These challenges 
resonate further with broader findings that effectiveness is highly dependent upon infrastructure 
designed to encourage organizational cohesion (Ciampa et al., 2023; Woodward & Thoma, 
2020). As such, leadership emerged as a critical factor in both strengthening and inhibiting 
coaching sustainability within the non-traditional setting.  
 The chapter additionally addressed the limitations of the study, including the single-site 
design, small sample size, and reliance on self-reported perceptions, which may influence 
transferability and the scope of interpretation. Nevertheless, strategies including triangulation, 
member-checking, reflexive journaling, and bracketing supported the credibility and 
NON-CLASSROOM-BASED LITERACY COACHING  110 
confirmability of interpretations. These measures of trustworthiness contributed to the overall 
transparency and interpretive rigor required for qualitative inquiry.  
 Lastly, the chapter outlined recommendations for future research and implications for 
leadership in NCBC contexts. Collectively, these recommendations highlight the need for 
expanded empirical inquiry into coaching across multiple sites, investigation of student 
achievement outcomes associated with coaching, and deeper exploration of coaching within 
virtual and hybrid modalities. The leadership implications emphasize the importance of 
protecting time for coaching, sustaining unified literacy frameworks, and promoting equity-
centered instructional practices.  
 In sum, the study contributes to the limited research base concerning literacy coaching in 
NCBC settings and offers meaningful insights into how coaching can be leveraged to strengthen 
instructional coherence, elevate teacher practice, and support student access in flexible learning 
environments. As NCBC school continue to expand and evolve, the findings presented here may 
guide leaders, practitioners, and researchers in designing literacy coaching systems that are 
sustainable, equitable, and responsive to the needs of both educators and students.
