ABSTRACT
While equity-, diversity-, and inclusion- (EDI) training has gained 
popularity in school settings, there is limited research on how to 
prepare individual school stav and whole schools to engage in 
such learning. Understanding antecedent factors for EDI learn-
ing is crucial for school psychologists who are engaged in 
systems-level consultation and aim to enhance school readiness 
for EDI initiatives. This qualitative study investigated the experi-
ences of elementary school educators who had just (nished 
a culturally responsive training series. Teacher experiences 
resulted in themes commensurate with the Collective and 
Individual Readiness Framework. Their retroactive insights are 
used to guide recommendations for school psychologists aim-
ing to improve school stav and overall school readiness for 
future culturally responsive and EDI professional learning 
initiatives.

Yet, when implementing EDI PL in schools, there can be a disconnect 
between training and practice, such that the training is not effective in reach-
ing its learning goals for teachers (Fallon et al., 2023). Several barriers con-
tribute to this gap, including a lack of school-wide commitment to EDI 
initiatives and school staff’s reluctance to engage with EDI PL content 
(Knox, 2025; Knox et al., 2023). Within the context of systems consultation, 
school psychologists can proactively address the individual- and school-level 
barriers to effective EDI PL, ensuring that educators and entire school com-
munities are better prepared to engage with and benefit from EDI initiatives. 
This calls attention to the critical importance of building readiness to provide 
high-quality training opportunities and promote authentic staff engagement 
in EDI PL. Readiness for EDI PL refers to the preparation of individual 
educators and schools to engage in EDI learning (Knox et al., 2023). The 
concept of readiness is underexplored in systems consultation, particularly as 
it pertains to designing and implementing EDI PL that advances equity in 
schools.
Thus, this study explores how school psychologists can leverage the experi-
ences of teachers after a culturally responsive training series to improve 
readiness for future EDI PL, which may be critical when engaging in systems 
consultation. In other words, by acknowledging the reflections and experi-
ences of teachers after EDI training, school psychologists can shape and 
enhance readiness for future PL through targeted, informed action in their 
systems consultation work.
School psychologists as EDI consultants
School psychologists are trained to consult with school staff and administra-
tors to enhance the implementation of evidence-based practices to meet the 
diverse needs of students (Sullivan et al., 2015). As culturally responsive and 
other EDI-based practices gain recognition for their potential to address the 
needs of historically minoritized students, school psychologists have been 
increasingly called upon to lead and support school staff in adopting these 
essential practices (Fallon et al., 2023). Much of the literature in this area 
focuses on school psychologists providing culturally responsive consultation, 
which acknowledges power imbalances between school psychologists and the

empower educators and promote shared decision-making (Castro-Villarreal & 
Rodriguez, 2017; Parker et al., 2020). Such consultation can also serve as 
a mechanism for discussing and dismantling inequitable school practices. 
For instance, Collier-Meek and colleagues (Collier-Meek et al., 2023) applied 
a critical lens to consultation to help school psychologists better advance 
equitable practices in schools, yet this work remains largely theoretical with 
few empirical studies demonstrating actual impact.
Although research has explored the mechanics and ethics of culturally 
responsive consultation, there is a notable gap in understanding how school 
psychologists prepare staff and school systems for learning about cultural 
responsiveness as part of formal EDI PL. Such preparation may rely on the 
expertise of school psychologists, which often spans consultation, EDI princi-
ples, and guiding organizational change. More research is needed to pinpoint 
the individual and systems-level factors that school psychologists can address 
to better support schools in effectively engaging with and benefiting from 
EDI PL.
Attention to readiness for EDI learning
For years, schools have implemented EDI PL to educate teachers on structural 
racism and its effects on minoritized students’ experiences in schools (Ash & 
Maguire, 2023; Fallon et al., 2023). More recently, EDI PL in schools have 
focused on increasing teachers’ cultural responsiveness – an approach rooted 
in the goals of EDI (Matschiner, 2023; McKoy et al., 2016). Despite the wide-
spread implementation of cultural responsiveness training and other EDI PL, 
mixed findings exist regarding its effectiveness in advancing greater educa-
tional equity (Austin et al., 2023; Bottiani et al., 2017). One reason for this 
ineffectiveness may be because EDI PL is often implemented with the assump-
tion that educators and schools are ready to engage with EDI content, as 
evidenced by the minimal attention to enhancing readiness for EDI learning 
when preparing for implementation. However, individual- and school-level 
readiness factors may facilitate or impede learning in EDI PL (Chung, 2013; 
Chung et al., 2016), highlighting the need to examine readiness further.
Research in other fields have indicated the importance of readiness in 
determining EDI PL outcomes (Chung, 2013; Chung et al., 2016). However, 
the educational and school psychology literature often describes attention 
to readiness as an afterthought through study implications rather than as 
central to research aims. For example, researchers may indicate learning 
about unearned privilege can cause resistance and discomfort among white 
participants, which contributes to reduced EDI PL effectiveness (Coles- 
Ritchie & Smith, 2017; Patton & Jordan, 2017). Similarly, Chang and 
Viesca (2022) stated that individuals may enter the training ready to discuss

cultural diversity but not ready to deconstruct the systemic racism that is 
inherent in school systems. Coll and Ferrer (2021) noted that EDI PL often 
includes sensitive topics, such as white privilege and the impact of slavery 
on racially minoritized students’ learning experiences. These topics may be 
challenging for some to discuss, particularly if attention to readiness to 
engage in EDI PL is skipped.
Lack of readiness for EDI PL among educators can result in resistance to 
EDI learning and simplistic conceptions of cultural responsiveness, which can 
result in potentially harmful practices for minoritized students (Sleeter, 2012). 
As another consequence of lack of readiness, Black and other racially minor-
itized educators may experience racial stress watching their colleagues engage 
reluctantly or superficially in such trainings (Coles-Ritchie & Smith, 2017; 
Patton & Jordan, 2017). Similarly, at the school-level, researchers have found 
that schools may not have the climate needed for educators to engage in 
authentic reflection and vulnerability with one another, or the leadership 
needed to regulate and direct EDI programs and practices, suggesting a lack 
of readiness to meaningfully implement such initiatives (Lac & Diamond,  
2019; Mosely, 2018; Patton & Jordan, 2017).
Under these conditions, engaging in and benefiting from EDI training can 
be challenging, and arguably, unrealistic. Given this, it is paramount for 
researchers to center readiness for EDI PL as a construct deserving of com-
prehensive examination. For school psychologists and external consultants, 
assessing and addressing readiness among educators and within the broader 
school context is essential to improving the effectiveness of EDI PL. 
Understanding educators’ existing EDI knowledge, attitudes, and prepared-
ness enables these professionals to collaborate with EDI training facilitators in 
designing interventions that address the specific needs and dynamics of the 
school environment. Furthermore, readiness serves as a framework for under-
standing variations in EDI PL outcomes, emphasizing the role of contextual 
factors in shaping school staff’s engagement with and experiences in training.
Theoretical framing
This study is informed by the Collective and Individual Readiness Framework 
(Knox et al., 2023; see Figure 1). The framework operates on the premise that 
understanding both individual and school-level readiness for EDI PL is essen-
tial for promoting effectiveness. To dismantle educational disparities, it is 
important to assess and attend to readiness before, during, and after training. 
This framework acknowledges that readiness is not static; it can fluctuate 
throughout the EDI learning journey and should be an ongoing focus during 
evaluation. The framework views readiness as a multidimensional construct 
and operates at two levels: collective (whole-school) and individual (school 
staff). The framework is also culturally responsive, incorporating perspectives

of minoritized school staff, whose insights on collective readiness may reveal 
needs overlooked by those in dominant positions within the school.
The collective level encompasses aspects of the school context that influence 
educators’ engagement – or lack thereof – in EDI PL. Collective readiness 
includes four interrelated domains: leadership, awareness, commitment, and 
climate. The leadership domain evaluates the extent to which the school has 
motivated and vulnerable changemakers in positions of power to champion 
EDI efforts. School leaders play a critical role in school change related to EDI 
PL, as their engagement can significantly influence teachers’ willingness to 
participate authentically in such initiatives (Palmer & Louis, 2017; Young 
et al., 2010). However, many school leaders may feel unprepared to lead 
these efforts, making leadership a vital domain to consider when developing, 
implementing, or evaluating EDI PL (Lac & Diamond, 2019; Swanson & 
Welton, 2019). This lack of preparation reflects a significant lack of diversity 
in leadership, which in turn limits the range of perspectives and lived experi-
ences informing school-wide initiatives, including EDI efforts. School leaders 
are overwhelmingly white as systemic racism hinders educators of color from 
ascending to leadership roles. Research indicates that educators of color 
frequently face barriers such as discriminatory hiring and promotion prac-
tices, hostile work environments, and limited access to mentorship and pro-
fessional development opportunities (Kohli, 2019). The absence of diverse 
leadership has far-reaching implications for school climate and the consulta-
tion work that occurs between and across staff. Leadership sets the tone for the 
school environment, influencing how staff engage with EDI initiatives, and

how they navigate sensitive and personal topics related to race and equity. 
Without diverse voices in leadership, schools may fail to create spaces where 
minoritized staff feel supported and empowered, leading to increased racial 
stress and feelings of isolation among these educators. This, in turn, can 
perpetuate inequities in school practices and hinder the effectiveness of EDI 
professional learning.
Awareness refers to the school’s recognition of the EDI-related needs of its 
students and staff, including an understanding of the historical marginaliza-
tion of racially minoritized families and its impact on current student needs. 
A lack of awareness – such as color-evasiveness – at the organizational level 
can obscure and perpetuate the realities of racial inequities within schools 
(Schofield, 2006). Commitment reflects the school’s acknowledgment of and 
response to the EDI-related needs of its community. This can range from 
minimal gestures, such as a shared diversity statement, to more meaningful 
actions, such as integrating cultural considerations into practices that affect 
racially minoritized students (Ramos Carranza et al., 2023). Climate assesses 
whether the school environment is conducive to open and trusting conversa-
tions about EDI. A competitive or hostile climate can inhibit educators’ will-
ingness to engage vulnerably with their peers, which is essential for effective 
professional development. Less readiness in the climate domain may lead to 
less effective EDI training, as educators may be reluctant to participate fully in 
discussions aimed at improvement (Cranston, 2011).
Individual readiness focuses on the personal factors that enable teachers to 
engage productively in EDI conversations with their colleagues. This level 
includes four domains: cultural, social, emotional, and behavioral. The cul-
tural domain involves a deep understanding of the broader systems of oppres-
sion that affect both self and others, aligning with cultural awareness 
constructs that emphasize continuous learning (Gay, 2018). When trainees 
begin EDI training with a belief that the foundational premises of EDI PL are 
flawed, or when they deny the existence of bias, the training is unlikely to be 
effective, regardless of its potential benefits (Chung, 2013). The social domain 
pertains to an educator’s skills and confidence in engaging with colleagues in 
EDI discourse, which can significantly impact their ability to benefit from the 
training. The emotional domain acknowledges the challenging nature of EDI 
learning, which often triggers self-blame and other negative reactions, and it 
focuses on the capacity of individual educators to manage these emotions 
during EDI PL. Finally, the behavioral domain is action-oriented, emphasizing 
the individual behaviors that prepare staff to engage in collective EDI 
discussions.
The Collective and Individual Readiness framework offers school psychol-
ogists a model to assess, monitor, and enhance readiness for EDI PL within 
schools. This framework allows school psychologists to identify and address 
readiness gaps at multiple levels, facilitating more effective planning,

Current study
Attending to individual and collective readiness for EDI PL is crucial for 
understanding educators’ experiences and outcomes following EDI imple-
mentation (Knox et al., 2023; Fallon et al., 2023). Despite the recognized 
importance of EDI PL, there has been limited attention to assessing and 
enhancing readiness before, during, and after such training. This study aims 
to address this gap by using the Collective and Individual Readiness 
Framework to explore how readiness for EDI learning emerges from educa-
tors’ experiences with EDI PL. We then use these findings to focus on how 
school psychologists, through consultation, can leverage educators’ reflections 
to improve readiness for future EDI learning opportunities. While the existing 
literature has examined various domains of the framework individually (e.g., 
Mosely, 2018; Palmer & Louis, 2017), this study is pioneering in its compre-
hensive application of all readiness domains with the school context.
Method
Study context
This study draws from a larger university–school partnership initiative aimed 
at providing professional learning on trauma-informed care to school districts 
in the southeastern United States. This extensive partnership involved over 
100 collaborators from higher education, K-12 schools, and related fields, such 
as mental health. The project encompassed comprehensive training for school 
staff on the impacts of trauma on learning and behavior, the implementation 
of tailored trauma-informed strategies at both school and classroom levels, 
and ongoing coaching to support effective practice integration. In collabora-
tion with an equity-based organization, the partnership incorporated 
a culturally responsive practices training series into the trauma-informed 
care program. The culturally responsive training began January 2021 and 
consisted of three 2-h online sessions via Zoom, occurring once per month. 
The PL series also included coaching sessions during the schools’ professional 
learning community meetings. The first workshop served as an introduction 
to equity work and consisted of terminology discussions, the history of equity 
work, and steps needed to achieve change within education. The second 
workshop focused on historical trauma, including causes and symptoms, 
and how it manifests in the school setting. The purpose of the third workshop

Procedure
Immediately after the completion of the culturally responsive professional 
development series, educators were invited to an individual interview via 
e-mail. Snowball sampling occurred resulting in a total of 12 teachers, 1 
principal, and 1 media specialist. For consistency, this study only uses data 
from the 12 teachers. Interviews were conducted by the first author and 
a graduate research assistant during and after the school day as well as on 
weekends to account for potential scheduling constraints. Each interview 
lasted between 30 min and 1 h via Zoom. Participants were each provided 
with a $50 Amazon gift card after interviews concluded. Several strategies were 
employed in pre-data collection to enhance the methodological rigor of the 
current study (Nowell et al., 2017). The original interview guide was piloted 
with three current elementary and middle school teachers to ensure that the 
questions asked were appropriately worded and elicited in-depth responses.
Positionality statement
In line with qualitative paradigms (Ladson-Billings, 2006), we recognize that 
researchers’ identities and cultural background cannot be separated from inter-
pretations of the data. As such, we offer insight into our positionalities as 
scholars. The lead author and principal investigator is a licensed psychologist 
with a doctorate in school psychology. She is a Black woman from a low- 
income background with interest and expertise in trauma-informed, culturally 
responsive practices and readiness for EDI PL. The second author is a white 
cisgender woman from the Midwestern United States, who is currently pursu-
ing her doctorate in school psychology. She has expertise in school-based EDI 
PL and a background in social psychological research. The third author is 
a Black cisgender woman who was raised in a single-parent low-income house-
hold within a mixed-income family. She is a licensed psychologist with lived, 
educational, and research experience on the inequities of educational practices 
and culturally responsive practices for marginalized and minoritized students 
and their families. The last author is a white cisgender woman who is a faculty 
member in a school psychology program in the Northeastern United States. She 
is a licensed psychologist and pursues research that explores decentering white-
ness in education through professional development in schools. Together, we 
used these positionalities to challenge thoughts about, make sense of, and open 
lines of inquiry into readiness for EDI PL as a multi-dimensional phenomenon.

Analytic plan
Interviews were transcribed verbatim using Zoom transcription software, and 
qualitative analysis was performed using Dedoose. The authors followed code-
book thematic analysis guidelines outlined by Braun and Clarke (2021) to 
engage in a critical examination of the data, which includes using a structured 
coding framework to make sense of the data, going through iterations of 
coding, and collating codes into coherent themes. A hybrid thematic analysis 
was employed wherein authors used a deductive approach to capture codes 
with the Collective and Individual Readiness Framework in mind while also 
permitting alternate messages to emerge from the data using inductive coding. 
As such, the authors allowed for a robust analysis of readiness within the data. 
As is characteristic of qualitative research, the goal was not to generalize 
findings to all educators, but rather to provide a rich, detailed understanding 
of the participants’ experiences and perspectives. This interpretive approach 
allows for an in-depth exploration of readiness within the specific context of 
this study.
The first draft of the codebook included codes commensurate with the 
Collective and Individual Readiness Framework. The first and second authors 
used this codebook to code 25% of the data. During this process, they 
examined the data with the assumption that other patterns may present 
themselves and warrant a new code. Data familiarization influenced authors’ 
movement between deductive and inductive modes as well as semantic and 
latent approaches. As a result, iterations of the codebook included secondary 
codes that seemed to describe the primary codes (i.e., readiness domains). The 
authors went through a process of coding all interviews and refining the codes 
to deepen their interpretation of the data. After the coding process was 
complete, the first and second authors went through a cycle of analyzing the 
data within codes to identify recurring and meaningful patterns that could be 
unified through themes. This process resulted in themes and sub-themes 
commensurate with the framework. All authors discussed the themes and 
their ability to accurately capture the data.
The authors did not engage in interrater reliability as it inherently 
assumes that multiple researchers assigning a code to the same piece of 
qualitative data is a meaningful measure of scientific rigor when it negates 
qualitative paradigm (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Instead, authors focused on 
interpretative depth (Braun & Clarke, 2022) and relied on peer debriefing 
to ensure authenticity of data analysis. The themes were presented to the 
primary researcher of the EDI PL who provided feedback and support for 
the accuracy of the themes. Peer debriefing helps increase the credibility of 
the analysis as it helps to verify data interpretations and encourages the 
coders to engage in reflexivity regarding potential influences on their 
interpretation of the data (Janesick, 2007). Additionally, researchers

Results
Consistent with the Collective and Individual Readiness Framework, two 
themes emerged in the data: (a) collective readiness and (b) individual readi-
ness. Participants’ comments reflected their collective and individual readiness 
for EDI PL and how their readiness informed their experiences with training. 
Notably, participants’ reflections often touched on multiple, interconnected 
aspects of readiness, highlighting how domains within the Collective and 
Individual Readiness Framework are not experienced in isolation.
Theme 1: collective readiness
Leadership
Relative to the other domains represented within the data, participants were 
reluctant to note how the prerequisites of leadership (e.g., priorities and 
encouragement) may have influenced their experiences within the culturally 
responsive professional development series. Those that did mention the 
importance of leadership noted the ways in which their school’s principals 
may have promoted conversations surrounding EDI. For example, Brittany 
noted that “my previous principal . . . was just not interested in pursuing 
equity work and I knew that this principal was and wanted to do the work at 
the school.” Bridget Jones stated that “she [principal] was real into equity and 
exploring that more,” but she also mentioned the ways in which leadership 
may have been limited due to other competing demands of the job (e.g., “I love 
her to death but I think she was just trying to survive. This was her first year of 
principalship. So, the equity committee didn’t really go anywhere this year, 
honestly”).
Commitment
Participants’ statements reflected the need for greater collective commit-
ment to facilitate EDI efforts. These responses often suggested that teachers

felt overwhelmed by the number of trainings provided, which undermined

behalf of their schools were based on their perceptions of the lack of actions 
taken to follow through with the content learned throughout the training. Bug, 
for instance, lamented that there was a need to create more tangible goals for 
improvement following conversations on EDI (e.g., “I think that it would have 
been kind of nice to do it in a small session and talk about making a goal for

strategies to her classroom (e.g., “Instead of feeling like it [the training] just

great”). Similarly, Oakley noted that competing obligations (e.g., a rigorous 
teaching schedule) served to undermine her ability to apply what she learned 
to her teaching (e.g., “I didn’t really have a chance to really do any of it or even 
be able to implement it like I want to”).
Interviewees’ comments that reflected collective commitment meaningfully 
intersected with comments regarding leadership. These comments suggest 
that a school’s commitment may be driven by those in leadership positions 
and how those individuals can communicate their support through actions 
that prioritize equity in crafting school initiatives. For example, Susan con-

Awareness
Similar to the leadership domain, collective awareness was a relatively under-
utilized code when examining participants’ reported experiences within the 
culturally responsive training. Interviewees who alluded to collective aware-
ness referred to the extent to which individuals within their school acknowl-
edged the need for conversations surrounding EDI. These responses included 
these statements from Country Girl:
I know that some people felt like it wasn’t necessary, and it was a waste of time, but this is 
something that I feel like every classroom, teacher, or every person that works in school 
could benefit from because we’re working with a diverse population. We, as a country 
and as a school, have to understand that the majority of our students are Hispanic and 
Black, so we need to take that into consideration when we’re hiring teachers and we’re 
hiring administrators, when we are doing our lesson plans and when we are working 
with families.
These responses suggest that although some teachers demonstrated individual 
awareness of the need to promote greater equity within their schools, a lack of 
collective awareness may have impeded the possible benefits of the training.
Climate
The final domain of collective readiness that was present within our data is 
that of climate. In general, participants’ comments regarding climate referred 
to the extent to which they felt that their training group was conducive to 
having productive conversations surrounding equity and social justice. These 
comments often noted that other individuals in their training setting were 
reluctant to participate, which likely communicated a lack of engagement in 
EDI efforts (e.g., “I know there were some people that never said one word” 
[Irma Note] and “I mean me and another girl felt free to talk, but there was 
another coworker that we worked with like she didn’t say anything” [Bridget 
Jones]).
Despite unequal participation among trainees within their groups, many

Theme 2: individual readiness
Social
Participants’ comments reflected elements of social readiness or the ability and 
efficacy to engage in productive interactions with colleagues regarding EDI 
efforts. In the current sample, researchers found evidence for the importance 
of participants’ social readiness and the ways in which it was related to 
individuals’ ability to be vulnerable with others. For example, Susan noted, 
“I think if we weren’t too afraid to speak up, it wouldn’t be as big of an issue . . . 
We just don’t know how to talk about it without feeling like we’re offending or 
we’re scared to say the wrong thing.” Such a comment suggests that minimal

social readiness may be related to a fear of being perceived negatively during 
EDI conversations.
Although being vulnerable in EDI PL can be challenging, many 
teachers’ comments suggested that they were willing to rise to this 
challenge in service of promoting more equitable outcomes for their 
students. These comments suggest adequate amounts of social readiness, 
which enabled the teachers to have meaningful conversations that could 
be leveraged in their work with students. Bug demonstrated her ability 
to reflect on her practices in conversations with other teachers in her 
group:
I had another student this year that was like “you always pick on me, you always call me

to really reflect on my own bias.
The only Black participant in the sample demonstrated the ability to speak up 
when needed to express her support for EDI efforts: “I [usually] just kind of sit 
back and observe and listen, but I did speak up . . . because the staff member 
that tried to push back against [the facilitator] is known for supporting 
politicians who are racist,” [Country Girl].
Emotional
The emotional domain was also quite prevalent among participants with many 
discussing the capacity (or lack thereof) to engage in reflection and grapple 
with the emotions that arise from EDI PL. Teachers noted the importance of 
readiness within the emotional domain: “I felt like for teachers . . . if you are 
not going to be reflective, you’re a deadweight,” (Bridget Jones). Similarly, Bug 
explained the need to be emotionally open and unguarded to promote change 
relevant to EDI efforts:
I think it just really pushed me out of my comfort zone and that’s what I needed. We 
need to be pushed if we’re gonna promote change, if we’re gonna change the way we’ve 
been doing things for years, we need to be uncomfortable.
Often, teachers noted the need to grapple with emotions that arise during EDI 
learning in their profession in order to create equitable spaces for students. 
However, several teachers demonstrated lower readiness in the emotional 
domain, which contributed to resistance to EDI PL. This resistance is illu-
strated in Susan’s experiences:
It kinda felt like being pushed against the wall sort of speak . . . I’m not that person 
they’re talking about and blaming . . . I was more defensive than I was actually being able

Cultural
Participants’ responses fell into the cultural domain when they noted their 
individual background and the ways in which this background influenced 
their understanding and awareness of EDI topics. As a demonstration of 
cultural readiness, one participant noted how her upbringing promoted 
greater readiness and motivation for EDI learning.
I grew up in another country and it really made me passionate about seeing the world in 
a different way . . . I’m very about different cultures and love getting know other students 
so I love really learning how I can implement that in my own classroom. (Bug)
At the same time, another participant from a different country noted how her 
background led to confusion regarding the need for talking about race during 
the EDI training. She states:
I think everything here is so focused on [race], so I really lost interest because it feels 
overwhelming for me. If I feel overwhelmed about them focusing on Black and White 
and all that stuff . . . like why is everything so focused on that, so I really lost interest 
during the workshops. (Mary)
These participants illustrated varying levels of readiness in the cultural domain 
as their being from other countries influenced their understanding of why EDI 
PL – with a focus on race in particular – is important. These comments 
illustrate how individuals vary in the ways in which they make meaning of 
their cultural background and how their experiences may be used to facilitate 
or impede EDI learning.
Other comments reflected how the location that one grows up in or 
inhabits influences cultural norms surrounding EDI topics. These com-
ments suggest that the cultural context in which EDI trainings take 
place may have important implications for the baseline readiness of 
trainees and how EDI messaging may be received. For example, Mary 
explained how the cultural understandings of individuals within the

Participants’ comments also meaningfully overlap between cultural and emo-
tional domains of individual readiness for EDI. These comments suggested the 
influence of minimal readiness within the cultural domain – or understanding of 
the need for EDI – on participants’ emotional reactions to the training. For 
example, Irma Note described, “Like it basically started out and they were saying

assumptions, as evidenced by the need for deemphasizing whiteness within the 
systemic oppression that stems from whiteness.

Discussion
There is a foundational yet overlooked question within consultation research 
and practice: How can educators learn from professional development, in this 
case EDI PL, if they are not ready to engage in and implement the content? 
Although previous research has highlighted the importance of readiness for 
EDI PL (Chung, 2013; Fallon et al., 2023; Knox, 2025), there has been limited 
exploration of this concept within the school context and how this information 
can be leveraged for school psychologists engaging in systems consultation. 
This study addresses this gap by identifying specific areas of readiness for EDI 
PL that are crucial for school staff.
Our findings contribute to the development of the Collective and Individual 
Readiness Framework (Knox et al., 2023) by detailing how various readiness 
factors influence experiences in EDI PL. By focusing on these identified 
readiness domains, school psychologists can better support schools in creating 
a more conducive environment for EDI PL. The following discussion inter-
prets the findings and describes how school psychologists can leverage their 
consultation practices to improve the planning and implementation of future 
EDI PL.
Absence of meaning making
Participants’ responses reflected frustration with their schools’ approaches to 
EDI initiatives, highlighting deficiencies in preparation, implementation, and 
follow-through. Many attributed these shortcomings to a lack of collective 
readiness within their schools, citing competing demands, limited actionable 
guidance, and weak accountability mechanisms as barriers to meaningful 
engagement with the training. These challenges collectively reflect an absence 
of meaning making within EDI efforts, as participants struggled to connect the 
training to substantive changes in their practices or school environments.
Leadership and commitment emerged as interconnected factors shap-
ing these experiences. While some participants acknowledged efforts by 
school leaders to prioritize equity, they also pointed to constraints such 
as limited capacity and competing responsibilities, which undermined 
sustained support for EDI initiatives. Teachers directly and indirectly 
expressed a need for leadership to establish clear goals, provide

[[p21]]
actionable follow-up, and model a commitment to EDI that extended 
beyond the training itself. In the absence of leadership support and 
organizational commitment to the importance of EDI efforts, teachers 
reportedly felt the training was delivered in a way that limited their 
ability to engage deeply with the content. Without impassioned and 
unwavering support from leadership, many teachers ultimately felt that 
the trainings represented a box to tick rather than a meaningful invita-
tion to transform school practices to better support minoritized stu-
dents. Greater readiness among school leaders could have addressed 
these gaps by emphasizing the importance of EDI learning, tailoring 
culturally responsive practices to the school’s context, maintaining 
momentum beyond the training sessions, and embedding equity princi-
ples into policies, procedures, and daily practices. Without such readi-
ness, the structural and cultural changes necessary to advance EDI 
efforts remain unrealized.
Self versus training
Teachers’ responses also highlighted how their individual readiness, particu-
larly in the emotional and cultural domains, influenced their engagement with 
EDI training. Teachers who recognized their cultural backgrounds and the 
need for cultural responsiveness generally found the training more accessible. 
In contrast, those who did not acknowledge their role within hegemonic 
systems struggled to engage meaningfully. Emotional readiness also varied; 
for example, many teachers used tactical words to describe the emotional 
responses they had in response to the EDI training. For example, white 
teachers described their stance as “defensive” as they felt “attacked” through-
out the training. The Black teacher, on the other hand, described EDI training 
as a “frustrating battle” in response to “White people’s anger.” These differ-
ences underscore that emotional and cultural readiness are intertwined and 
affect how teachers respond to EDI learning, impacting their willingness to 
engage deeply with the content.
These domains of individual readiness likely exert mutual influence on one 
another because the ways in which we respond emotionally to content is 
wrapped up in who we are as individuals, our lived history, and cultural 
norms. Together, an individual’s cultural experiences and emotional responses 
shape the extent to which they are receptive to EDI learning; teachers may be 
willing to embrace the EDI training, or they may “shut down” and “feel taken 
aback” when provided with the opportunity for critical reflection. In this way, 
cultural and emotional readiness may influence the extent to which teachers 
feel willing to approach or avoid EDI learning, as reflected in sentiments that 
express a “self versus training” mentality.

Engaging in silence or vulnerability
Collective and individual readiness do not operate in a vacuum – there is 
meaningful interaction between these levels to consider. In particular, tea-
chers’ responses suggested the bidirectional relationship between the climate 
and social domains of Collective and Individual Readiness Framework. These 
domains worked together to set the tone for social interactions and created 
a space in which teachers felt afraid to speak up or empowered to lead difficult 
conversations surrounding EDI learning.
Many teachers’ comments suggested that the extent to which they 
were willing to engage socially was influenced by others. These com-
ments reflected the importance of collective climate for creating 
a context where other teachers felt comfortable sharing their experi-
ences, verbalizing their feelings, and engaging in conversation with 
others. The school’s climate suggests the extent to which there is rela-
tional trust among school staff, which is an important component of 
collective readiness for EDI learning. Without relational trust in the 
social environment of a school, individuals may be fearful to share 
their perspectives, be open with one another, and participate fully in 
the EDI training. When the school climate is trusting, supportive, and 
safe, school staff may feel more inclined to be courageous in their 
conversations with one another, to challenge each other in EDI learning, 
and support their collective growth toward greater equity.
The social environment of a school is, of course, a reflection of the 
individuals who comprise the school staff. In this way, collective culture 
is made up of the social dispositions of individuals and the interactions 
that occur among these individuals. Within the context of the Collective 
and Individual Readiness Framework, this connection suggests the 
importance of attending to individual readiness in the social domain 
when considering the extent to which a school climate is conducive to 
critical conversations during EDI learning. Many teachers expressed 
a fearlessness in their ability to continue critical conversations through-
out EDI learning, even when they were met with silence or resistance on 
behalf of other teachers. Other teachers expressed a desire to engage in 
conversation, but an unwillingness to be the only teacher who spoke up. 
These comments reflect how collective and individual readiness may 
diverge within the context of social interactions. Teachers with high 
levels of confidence regarding their ability to talk with others about 
EDI may be able to engage in conversation, even in the absence of 
a conducive climate. Other teachers, however, may lack this confidence 
and struggle to deploy their conversational skills when a school climate 
needs greater receptivity to EDI topics.

Implications
These findings offer several implications for consultation. First, consultants 
should focus on enhancing collective readiness by emphasizing the crucial role 
of strong leadership and commitment in advancing EDI efforts. For instance, 
consultants (e.g., school psychologists) can consult with school leaders to 
develop and implement a clear, actionable EDI vision that includes specific 
goals and ongoing support structures for staff. This might involve facilitating 
conversations or training sessions with school leadership that equip adminis-
trators with strategies to champion EDI effectively and integrate EDI priorities 
into everyday school practices (leadership). By working closely with leadership 
to establish and maintain these structures, school psychologists can help 
ensure that EDI initiatives are not only articulated in policy but actively 
practiced and supported throughout the school community (commitment). 
This approach may strengthen educators’ assessment of the school’s commit-
ment, making EDI PL more meaningful for educators.
School psychologists might also make clear the goals and implications of future 
EDI PL (i.e., positive student outcomes), what the EDI PL content entails, and 
potential areas of contention for those with lesser cultural and emotional readi-
ness. They can then use multicultural consultation (Newman & Ingraham, 2020) 
to encourage educators to engage in individual work exploring their history, 
identity, values, and priorities (e.g., where they were raised, where they grew up, 
the political climate around them; cultural) to be self-aware and subsequently 
open about who they are and how they might grow in the context of training. They 
can also encourage educators to think about and prepare for how they might be 
vulnerable and have a planned response for how they could react should their 
views be challenged in a training space (e.g., listen and reflect versus being 
defensive; emotional). Finally, they can encourage educators to reflect on how 
they may “show up” in EDI PL by reflecting on their common verbal (e.g., how 
they phrase their thoughts, the typical volume of their voice, the amount of taking 
they typically do) and non-verbal (e.g., facial expressions and eye contact) beha-
vior to convey an openness and willingness to engage in EDI training content 
(behavioral). Importantly, the school psychologist is simply encouraging these acts 
and is not responsible for ensuring individuals’ compliance.
School psychologists should work with school leaders to foster a positive 
and trusting school climate that encourages open dialogue about EDI issues 
(climate). They can facilitate workshops or small group discussions to build 
relational trust. They can also advocate for safe space groups and have some-
one with a shared identity facilitate these groups to ensure a more relatable and 
supportive environment that allows educators to develop their confidence in 
discussing difficult topics (social).
School psychologists are pivotal in advancing EDI initiatives in schools; 
however, their success in this role depends on their critical consciousness and 
JOURNAL OF EDUCATIONAL AND PSYCHOLOGICAL CONSULTATION 21

[[p24]]
ability to engage in self-reflective practices (Ash & Maguire, 2023). Critical 
consciousness requires ongoing examination of one’s positionality, privilege, 
and biases, as well as a willingness to recognize when collaboration or deferral 
is necessary to ensure culturally responsive and equitable practices 
(McKenney, 2021). For example, a school psychologist who recognizes that 
their expertise does not fully encompass the lived experiences of certain 
student or staff populations may seek collaboration with colleagues or external 
experts to inform their approach. This reflective approach strengthens the 
school system’s capacity to implement meaningful EDI efforts without risking 
harm from unacknowledged gaps in understanding.
Finally, readiness consultation may also be challenging for school psychol-
ogists from minoritized groups who might face their own cultural and emo-
tional hurdles in this work. However, these psychologists bring unique 
strengths, such as lived experiences and deep insights into systemic inequities, 
which can significantly enrich consultation efforts. To navigate challenges, 
school psychologists should be mindful of their preparedness to consult with 
educators and engage in ongoing self-reflection to ensure their approaches are 
both culturally responsive and sustainable. Seeking collaboration or support 
from school counselors, community-based EDI experts, or professional net-
works should be seen as a strength-based strategy that fosters shared account-
ability and continuous growth. These collaborations not only support the 
individual school psychologist but also enhance the effectiveness of EDI 
initiatives by bringing in diverse perspectives and expertise.
Limitations and future research
This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting 
the findings. For example, snowball sampling approach is a limitation as the 
participants may have, inherently, been more ready to talk about EDI issues and 
participate in the interview. This produces an examination of readiness from the 
perspective of teachers who are perhaps more ready as compared to others.
Another notable limitation of the sample is the lack of diversity in the 
participant pool, which was primarily composed of White, female elementary 
school teachers. While this reflects the demographic composition of the U.S. 
K-5 educator workforce, it limits the ability to explore readiness domains from 
a broader range of perspectives, particularly those of racially and ethnically 
minoritized educators or individuals with different gender identities. Future 
research should intentionally recruit participants from a wider array of racial, 
ethnic, gender, and professional backgrounds to capture a more comprehen-
sive understanding of readiness for EDI learning.
Although Knox et al. (2023) believe readiness should be acknowledged and 
studied before, during, and after EDI PL, within the context of this study, we 
only studied readiness after. Although this study is the first to our knowledge

within the education field to center readiness for EDI learning within the 
evaluation of an EDI PL, future research should examine readiness prior to 
and during the implementation of EDI PL.
Conclusion
Researchers and practitioners should attend to the individual and school con-
textual prerequisites that can impede or facilitate EDI PL effectiveness – called 
readiness for EDI learning. This study is the first of its kind to use the Collective 
and Individual Readiness Framework to examine how individual and collective 
factors can impact educators’ experiences in an EDI PL. Findings provided 
evidence for the eight readiness domains and information on how school 
psychologists can work within schools to increase readiness for future EDI PL. 
As schools work toward greater equity and inclusion, the role of school psychol-
ogists in guiding and supporting these processes will be instrumental in achiev-
ing impactful and sustained progress.
